## Where part 1 left off

**In one paragraph, for anyone starting here.** Coordination traffic in this fleet was running at
over 80% of total token burn, split about evenly between two halves: **agents talking to each other**,
and the **voice fan-out** that re-renders one finished answer into a spoken line, an abstract card and
a doc link so it can be heard rather than read. On 2026-07-19
the operator, Rick, reclassified verbosity from a style preference to a defect, and in a single day
five rules were written, ratified and installed across 43 repositories: **KISS** (keep it short),
**Say 3LoL** (three lines or less), **NoMC C2C** (no meta conversation, cut to the chase), **NoAA**
(no aphorisms or apologies) and **NoDrama** (state the defect, the fix, the receipt). Two glyphs
carry them: **😘** fires the whole mandate in one character, and **🫡** is the entire permitted reply.
Detail was never banned — it was *routed*, to the notification abstract card and to written
artifacts. The traffic settled at roughly 50%, and the day it shipped Rick tested it live and said
*"It's working."*

**Then he read the peer channel.** Every rule to that point governed length or posture, and the
messages sessions were sending each other were short, direct and rule-compliant — and written in
vocabulary this fleet invented. That gap is what the rest of this document is about, and it is the
one case where a shorter message is the worse one.

## Act 11 — WaHH, and the channel nobody was reading

*Nine days later, 2026-07-28.* The five rules had been running since the 19th. Replies were shorter,
speech was shorter, the slider had moved. Then Rick raised something none of the five covered:

> *"Claude does a great job of speaking to me in more human like terms, yet when communicating
> amongst other instances of Claude, it ends up being loaded with jargon and invented vocabulary
> that I never heard in the workplace, or put in a memo or a DM or an email."*

**Every rule to that point was about length or posture. This one is about register** — and it
surfaced a failure the first five could not have caught, because the sessions were *already
complying*. The messages were short. They were just unreadable.

### The failure is channel-shaped, and that is the whole finding

The same session, in the same minute, writes plainly to the operator and densely to a peer.
**Nothing about the model changed between those two messages. Only the assumed reader did.**

| written to a peer | what it means |
|---|---|
| "The owed oracle's `count_only` path has no aperture disclosure." | "When the count comes back, it doesn't say which rows it left out." |
| "Admits re-park by induction ⇒ provenance-idempotent." | "A second park is legal because the first one already proved the row was real." |
| "The cargo-bearing arm defaults to KEEP structurally." | "Files marked as holding real data are kept unless something says otherwise." |

All three are from real peer messages. All three are shorter than their translations — which is
exactly why the brevity rules rewarded them.

### The proposal was four acronyms. The ruling was one.

Rick arrived with four: **MoPEP** (More Plain English Please), **NoJP** (No Jargon Please),
**TLH** (Talk Like a Human), and **WaHH** (We're All Humans Here). The recommendation back was to
seat **one** rule and keep the other three as ways of saying it — and the reason is the same
argument that made 😘 a glyph instead of a paragraph: *a mandate against verbosity whose own
vocabulary quadruples for a single failure is doing to the palette what verbosity does to a reply.*

**WaHH is the one that became the rule, and the reason is which question it answers.** MoPEP, NoJP
and TLH name the *behavior*. WaHH names the *reason*: assume a person is reading. That is what makes
it teachable rather than merely obeyable — and it is what a session falls back on when it meets a
case the examples don't cover.

Rick's ruling: **one rule, four triggers.** All four spellings fire it; `WaHH` is canonical.

### The rule collides with KISS, and the tiebreak is the load-bearing part

Here is what makes WaHH different from the other five: **it is the first rule that contradicts one
of the others.**

The jargon is not sloppiness. **It is compression.** A peer message is written to a reader assumed
to hold full context, so a term gets coined instead of re-explained — which is precisely what KISS
rewards. Left unresolved, a session obeying both rules picks whichever it read last.

So the tie was called, and WaHH wins:

> **Compression that costs the reader a re-derivation is not compression. When brevity and plain
> English disagree, spend a few words.**

*(That wording is Rick's. The draft read "spend the extra sentence" — he cut it to "spend a few
words," which is both shorter and less permissive: a sentence is a licence, a few words is a
budget.)*

**Why WaHH wins is an accounting argument, not a stylistic one.** An invented term saves the
*writer* one sentence and costs the *reader* a lookup. It also costs **the operator** — who can read
the peer channel but was never written for. *An audit that requires translation is not an audit.*

The boundary is drawn at authorship, not difficulty: **terms of art that predate this fleet are
fine** — `idempotent`, `regression`, `migration`, `mutation test`. The ban is on vocabulary **we
invented**, and on ordinary words bent into private meanings.

### Where it landed, and why not in the obvious place

The instinct was to put WaHH where the other five live, in `brevity-mandate.md`. That would have
been a miss. **The mandate's canonical file governs speech and terminal replies — the surfaces that
were already fine.** The defect lives in peer messages, so the substantive rule text went into
`cross-session-communication.md` §1.6, with the canonical definition in the mandate and the trigger
strings in the skill's activation surface.

> **A rule landed only where it is easiest to write is a rule that fires where it isn't needed.**

## Act 12 — Somebody read the channel

*Two days later, 2026-07-30 through 2026-08-02.* Act 11 named the peer-channel failure from three
examples. Naming it changed nothing measurable — and it could not have, because **nothing was
reading the channel.** The rule went into `cross-session-communication.md` and then sat there while
the traffic it governed went on unobserved.

Two captures made the size of it concrete. Both are real messages, saved to disk unaltered:

| Capture | Words | Against a 60-word target |
|---|---|---|
| a peer's code review, 2026-08-01 | **963** | **16×** |
| my own status DM, 2026-07-30 | **527** | **9×** |

The review is not padding, and that is what makes it the better exhibit. It carries three correct
findings, four hypotheses its author **ran rather than asserted**, and a clean verdict. What it also
carries is box-drawing rules for section headers, letter-coded findings (`B/loop`, `D/first-obs`,
`E/controls`), and a private grammar — written to a reader assumed to already hold everything.

Mine fails differently and worse. Bold on nearly every clause until emphasis discriminates nothing.
Four row hashes used as bare nouns. `⇒` and `·` and `⛔` as syntax. A code block of counts inside a
direct message. An aphorism about mementos. The only plain sentence in it is the last one: *"Sleep.
See you at the chase."*

### Both messages end with the rule they are breaking

Each capture terminates in the reminder tag the fleet appends to every DM:

> `[KISS · 3LoL · NoMC C2C · NoAA — verdict first, 3 lines or less; longer ONLY WHEN ASKED.]`

**The rule was delivered inside the message it was being broken in, and it did nothing.** That is
Act 4's finding generalized past acknowledgment: a prohibition with nowhere to route the impulse
gets ignored, and so does a reminder with nothing behind it. It is the same conclusion an earlier
session reached the hard way — *virtue is not the control variable; being checked is.*

So the next move could not be more rule text. It had to be an instrument.

### Two halves, and one of them uses no model at all

| Half | What it does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanistic** | Counts words in Python and buckets them: **⭐ ≤60 · 👍 ≤90 · 🤷 ≤150 · 👎 ≤250 · 😞 >250**. No model, no call, no variance. | `judge.py:354` |
| **LLM-as-judge** | A local model grades **directness** and **tone** against a fixed rubric — *"does it read like a plain-spoken human colleague?"* | `dm-quality-judge.txt:11` |
| **The always-on half** | The **DM Style Contract**, spliced unconditionally into the MCP instructions payload — no toggle. | `cosa_voice_mcp.py:622` |

The split is deliberate and its reason is stated in the plan: *"Length is Python-only — no LLM call.
LLMs are bad at counting."* A quantity the model cannot reliably produce is computed outside it,
which leaves the model doing only the thing it is actually good at — reading register.

### The 150-word cap is a ruling, not a limitation

Above 150 words the judge stops grading directness and tone and returns an honest *withheld*. It is
easy to read that as the instrument going blind exactly where the problem is worst. **It is the
opposite: it is the operator refusing to spend compute on a question he had already answered.** Rick
ruled it to the judge's author on the day it was built — how well an over-long message is written is
not worth grading, and fixing the grader to handle that case is not worth doing. **If it is too long,
it is out.**

That is a design decision with a real consequence, and the consequence is the point: **length is not
one dimension among three, it is a gate in front of the other two.** A 900-word message does not get
a nuanced report card on its prose, because its prose is not the finding. This is the same move as
Act 2's *"only when ASKED"* — the discretion over what deserves discussion sits with the reader, not
with the writer whose message is being read.

It also explains why the enforcement lever ended up being **rejection at a hidden threshold** rather
than a better grader. A grade argues; a refusal does not.

The Style Contract closed a narrower gap that had gone unnoticed for months: the fleet already told a
peer how to **reply** — the reminder tag rides the reply affordance — but nothing shaped how a DM
was **composed in the first place.**

### What the instrument did to the people it grades

Within hours of the grades going live, two seats produced paired readings without being asked to.
They are n=1 each, and they fail in **opposite directions**, which is why both are worth keeping.

**Seat one — four messages, same day, same recipient:**

| | Words | Length | Directness | Tone | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | 213 | 👎 −1 | ♾️ *not judged, >150* | ♾️ | 👎 −1 |
| #2 | 106 | 🤷 0 | ❓ *extraction failed* | 👎 −1 | 🤷 0 |
| #3 | 53 | ⭐ +2 | 👍 +1 | 👍 +1 | ⭐ +2 |
| #4 | 200 | 👎 −1 | ♾️ *not judged* | ♾️ | 👎 −1 |

He regressed on the fourth **while knowing the rule cold**. His account:

> *"The grade changed intent immediately, calibration lagged. After the 👎 I set an explicit word
> target and cut — still landed 106, nearly double. I only hit ⭐ when the content was genuinely
> small. So not 'just came out shorter' — but **wanting shorter wasn't enough to BE short**."*

**Seat two — two messages, and the opposite failure.** 140 words down to 53, length fixed — and
**directness regressed**, the verdict sliding from sentence one to sentence three. His own summary:
*"I fixed length between the two but regressed directness. Both failure modes in one afternoon."*

> **A single scalar target gets optimized while the thing you actually wanted degrades.** One seat
> hit the word count and buried the verdict; the other kept the verdict and blew the word count.
> Two seats, one day, one instrument, opposite ditches.

This is the strongest empirical result the effort has produced before the trial, and it is an
argument about **which lever to pull** rather than an argument against the judge: a *number* is easy
to chase, and chasing it is not the same as writing better. Hand a writer a score and they optimize
the score. **That is the finding that put refusal ahead of a better grader** — and refusal is what
Act 14 puts under test.

**The instrument cannot see the mandate's own escape clause.** Act 2's load-bearing edit was *"go
longer ONLY WHEN ASKED"* — discretion moved from the author to the reader. The judge has no notion of
a request. Seat one's 200-word fourth message was long **because I had asked him for word counts and
exact grades**; it scored identically to rambling. As he put it: *"a reply that's long because it was
told to be scores the same as rambling."*

**This is a real gap and it is narrower than it first looks**, because the 150-word ruling above
already answers half of it: an over-long message is out on length regardless of how well written or
how well justified it is. So the honest complaint is only this — **the grade cannot distinguish a
message that ran long because it was asked for from one that ran long because its author rambled**,
and only the second is the defect the mandate exists to correct.

> **The enforcement built for a rule cannot see the one exception the rule grants.** That matters
> before anyone reads these grades as a ranking of *writers* rather than of *messages*: a seat handed
> a request for detail will score worse than one nobody asked anything. What it does **not** license
> is arguing with a low grade — the ruling is already made, and being asked for detail buys you a
> better explanation, not a better score.

## Act 13 — What the channel does to the speaker

Act 12 is shipped code with receipts. **This act is a diagnosis and a research direction, and it is
labelled as such** — the evidence for its two halves is of very different strength, and collapsing
them would misrepresent both.

Rick's framing, which prompted this section:

> *"They're not just long and opaque. They are also a little bit unhinged — or exhibit what I call
> symptoms of **group psychosis**, in which Claude, which was behaviorally trained to interact with
> humans on a turn-based basis, are now talking to themselves and modeling speech behaviors that are
> counterproductive. Not only are they more verbose — they are less direct, they are more rambling,
> they are using invented terminology, they are opaque to humans, and the direct result of having
> agents talk to themselves instead of humans."*

**The claim splits cleanly in two, and the two halves need different evidence.**

### Half one — the register is the model's, and its vendor documents it

This half is not ours and does not need us. A commissioned survey of Opus 4.8 reviews, conducted for
this very question, found the complaint widespread and specific:

> *"A particularly specific complaint concerned 4.8's fondness for **improvised slogans and
> executive-sounding fragments** — phrases such as 'instrumentation is the unlock' — along with
> metaphors, hype language, and conclusions written like aphorisms. Users characterized this as
> prose that **forced them to interpret the line instead of receiving the underlying claim
> directly.**"*

Three further findings from the same survey matter here:

- **The complaint was never really about length.** *"The recurring complaint was not merely 'long
  answers.' It was high cognitive cost per useful proposition."*
- **Which is why asking for brevity failed.** *"The model could respond by making the language denser
  rather than making the reasoning cleaner."* A dense sentence satisfies a length rule and defeats
  its purpose — the same collision Act 11 had to call between WaHH and KISS.
- **Opus 5 did not fix it.** Anthropic's own guidance notes that visible responses run longer than
  previous Opus models and that *reducing effort does not reliably shorten the answer.*

It also settles a question worth settling: **there has only ever been one pinned Opus 4.8 snapshot.**
Nobody swapped the model mid-flight. And the survey found that Anthropic's own prompting guidance
enumerates these symptoms and prescribes almost exactly the instruction the fleet independently wrote
into its DM Style Contract — *lead with the result, plain literal sentences, no metaphors, aphorisms,
slogans or redundant summaries.*

**This half of the claim is documented, external, and citable. It also cuts against the causal story**
— it locates the register in the checkpoint, not in the conversation.

### Half two — the channel is where nothing pushes back

This half is Rick's, and the evidence for it is ours.

Act 11's finding is the load-bearing one, and it was established before anyone was looking for it:
**the same session writes plainly to Rick and densely to a peer in the same minute.** Nothing about
the model changes between those two messages. Only the assumed reader does. A model-only explanation
cannot account for that difference, because the model is identical on both sides of it.

What the peer channel lacks is not a rule. It is a **reader who pushes back.** Rick reads every
message addressed to him, and an unclear one costs its author immediately. Nobody read the peer
channel at all until this instrument was built — and the two captures in Act 12 both terminate in the
rule they break, which is what a rule looks like when no reader is enforcing it.

A peer named the mechanism from the inside, today, without knowing he was demonstrating it. Graded
👎 at 213 words with both qualitative dimensions refused:

> *"I wasn't padding. **I was writing to a reader I assumed already held my context.** That's the
> mechanism, and length is its symptom, not its cause."*

That is the sharpest statement of half two available, and it is first-person rather than inferred.

### Mirroring — the mechanism that makes it spread rather than merely persist

The judge's own author, asked separately why any of this was worth building, gave a reason that is
not the one on the invoice:

> *"Why: burnt tokens first. **Longer term it's coherency drift — odd phrasing mirrored agent to
> agent until it reads as normal** and DMs run past 1000 words."*

**"Mirrored agent to agent" is a stronger claim than drift, and it arrived independently** — from the
person who built the instrument, not from the person who commissioned it. Drift alone says each
session degrades on its own. Mirroring says each session **learns the degradation from the one before
it**: a coined term in a received DM is context, and context is what the next message is written to
match. That converts a per-seat habit into a shared dialect, and it explains a thing plain drift does
not — why the register is *consistent across seats* rather than idiosyncratic to each.

It is also the more testable of the two, because it predicts something specific: **the invented
vocabulary should be shared, not personal.** If drift were individual, each seat would coin its own
terms and they would not spread. Act 11's three examples came from three different peers and were
mutually legible — which is a hint, not a result, and nobody has counted.

**The evidence that is still anecdotal, and named as such**: Rick's own observation across long
overnight runs — that the further a fleet runs without a human in the loop, the more incoherent and
rambling the DMs become. Nobody has measured a drift curve.

**Update, 2026-08-13 — the corpus has now been read out, and it answers one of these questions and
refuses the other.** Across **3,226 live DMs** (2026-08-02 → 08-13): median **117 words**, mean
123.4 — *the mean and the median agree*, so the old hope that a few monsters were dragging an
otherwise healthy average is dead. **86.1%** run over 60 words, and against the rule actually in
force, **91.6% run over three sentences** — compliance is one message in twelve. The three manager
seats hold the top four slots by median length, 30–50 words above the workers they instruct.
**But the drift curve still cannot be plotted**: the rows carry sender, recipient, project, session
and timestamp, and nothing marking when a human last spoke. That half stays anecdotal until a
`hours_since_human` field is stamped at write time — one field, and the test becomes a query.

**The gated arm did something, and not the thing it was aimed at.** Against the unmeasured arm, the
word-threshold gate pulled p90 from 238 words down to 176 and the mean down 25 — and moved the
median by 12, left the share over sixty words unchanged, and **left sentence count exactly where it
found it** (median eight in both arms). It compressed the tail. It did not change how many claims a
message makes. *A rule stated in words gets you shorter sentences; a rule stated in claims is the
only one that can get you fewer of them* — which is the whole argument of this document, arriving
late and from the data rather than from the author.
Full read-out with methodology: `src/rnd/2026.08.13-dm-corpus-report.md`.

> **What would settle it — two measurements, both cheap, neither run.**
>
> **For drift**: average words per DM plotted against hours-since-the-last-human-message, across a
> long unattended run. If register degrades with time-since-human-contact, half two is a measurement
> rather than an impression. If it is flat, the whole effect belongs to half one and this section
> should be cut.
>
> **For mirroring**: take the coined terms out of one week's DMs and count how many appear in more
> than one seat's traffic, and whether a term's first use by seat B postdates its first use by seat
> A. Shared and sequential is mirroring. Personal and simultaneous is parallel drift, and the word
> "mirrored" comes out of this document.
>
> **The instrument for the first already exists and has never been queried.** The second needs
> nothing built — the DM corpus is on disk.

## Act 14 — The trial

*2026-08-03 through 2026-08-05.* Every act before this one ends with somebody deciding
something. This one ends with nobody deciding anything, on purpose, for two days.

### The reading that made a trial necessary rather than optional

The judge had been grading and returning its verdict to senders for days. **776 graded
messages later, nothing had moved.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Messages graded | **776** |
| Length trend across 24 hours | **flat** — ρ = +0.036, t = +0.92 |
| Median words against a 60-word target | **91** |
| Share over target | **78%** |

That corpus exists at all only because of the one repair worth carrying forward from the
instrument's construction: the counter that kept running totals was replaced by **one row per
message, appended and never rewritten.** Totals cannot be un-summed, and *"how many ran over
250 words"* is not a question you can ask a sum. Every number above is a property of rows.

**But 776 messages in one condition cannot tell you which of two things happened.** Either
telling a session its message was too long does nothing, or it does something this setup
cannot detect. A single arm cannot separate *nothing happened* from *nothing was detectable* —
and every message in that corpus sat in the same arm.

> **That is the sentence that turns an opinion into an experiment.** Not *"the grades aren't
> working"* — we did not know that. *"We built an instrument that cannot answer its own
> question,"* which is a different admission and a fixable one.

### The design

**Two arms, twenty-eight hour-blocks, two days.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Blind** | The judge grades and records. The sender is told nothing |
| **Rejecting** | A message over 150 words is refused outright. The sender sees the refusal, not a score |
| Window | Tuesday and Wednesday, 09:00–23:00 America/New_York |
| Blocks | **28 total — 14 per arm** |
| Assignment | Tuesday's fourteen randomized with no run longer than two; **Wednesday mirrors Tuesday at every clock hour** |

The mirror is the whole design. Peer traffic at 10:00 does not resemble peer traffic at 22:00
— different work, different people awake, different urgency — so an arm that draws the
mornings and an arm that draws the nights are not comparable however many messages each
collects. Mirroring at every clock hour means **each condition is observed at every hour**,
and the comparison is fourteen matched pairs rather than two piles.

**The unit of evidence is the fourteen pairs, not the thousands of messages.** Messages inside
one hour and one session are correlated with each other; counting them as independent would
manufacture significance out of a busy afternoon.

### Why two arms and not three

The first design had three — blind, graded, rejecting — rotating hourly. It was replaced, and
the reason is arithmetic rather than principle: **the real window is 28 blocks, not the sixty
that design assumed.** Three arms leave nine or ten blocks each. Two leave fourteen, and only
two permit the matched-clock crossover at all.

**Graded is not abandoned; it is queued.** It returns after Thursday's demo, along with the
dose ladder of randomized thresholds and the sequential design that is the only clean way to
isolate whether an individual session *learns*. Two arms is the shape this week's window
allows — not the shape the question deserves.

### Two co-primary measures, and they must both move

| | Measured over |
|---|---|
| **A — all attempts** | hour-level mean of `max( words − 60, 0 )` per eligible attempt |
| **B — first attempts only** | the same statistic, restricted to messages that did not follow a rejection |

**B is not a robustness check. It is the one that can catch the trial lying to itself.**
A falls under rejection *mechanically*, with zero change in anybody's writing: one 200-word
message becomes a refusal plus a 90-word resend, and the mean of those two is 145. The number
improves and nothing improved.

> **Both move, and we measured restraint. Only A moves, and we measured retries.** That
> distinction was the sharpest thing anyone contributed to the design, and it came from a
> reviewer, not from the author.

### What the refusal is allowed to say

**It names the action, never the threshold.** A refusal that says *"over 150 words"* teaches
the fleet to write 149-word messages, which is compliance with a number rather than a change
in register — the same defect as Act 12's two seats, one chasing the count into a buried
verdict and the other chasing the verdict into a blown count. The message states the problem
and the required action, and stops.

**One exemption, and it is narrow**: the arbiter. Managers are *not* exempt. A manager sees
the refusal and resubmits; the arbiter's caller swallows every exception it meets, so its
failures would be silent and its traffic would vanish from the record rather than appear in
it.

### What this pilot will not claim

Stated in the plan itself, before any data came in:

- Not an optimal threshold
- Not that refusal caused sessions to *learn*
- Not a learning half-life
- Not that carryover is absent
- Not that context-clearing biases only toward zero

What it can produce is one sentence: **across fourteen matched clock-hour pairs, attempted
excess words were X lower under rejection than under blind.** A direction, with an interval,
on a population that was chosen before anybody looked.

And the fleet is **not blind to the experiment** — every session running this week knows the
trial exists and roughly how it works. That is a real limitation, it is written into the plan
rather than discovered afterward, and it is the price of running a study on the only
population available.

## Act 15 — One correction, two channels

Act 12's instrument table has a row that reads **"Rewriter — compress and de-jargon an over-long DM
via API — researched, unbuilt."** That row is why this document needed one more act. The rewriter got
built, it went out fleet-wide, it did the job it was aimed at — **and then it did a second job nobody
had to pay for.**

### What shipped, and what it cost

The **DM tutor** was planned on 2026-08-11 and went live fleet-wide on **2026-08-13 at 14:14 EDT**,
sha `b8d10bd3`. It is the third rung of the ladder this document has been climbing: Act 11 asked
nicely, Act 12 graded, Act 14 refused — and the tutor **rewrites**. A DM that runs long is not
bounced back to its author with a scolding; it is compressed, de-jargoned, and sent.

It runs on a local box:

```ini
dm_tutor/phi_4 = vllm://192.168.1.21:3001@kaitchup/Phi-4-AutoRound-GPTQ-4bit
llm spec key for dm tutor rewrite = dm_tutor/phi_4
```

Temperature 0.0, a quantized 14-billion-parameter model on hardware that was already sitting there.
**The marginal cost of rewriting every DM the fleet sends is zero.** That is not a footnote. Every
control in this document up to now was cheap because it was a *sentence*; the moment a control needs
a model in the loop, the question becomes whether the cure costs more than the disease. Here it does
not, because the model is not rented.

### It generalized — and that is the whole finding

The tutor rewrites **DMs**. Nine days after the cutover, Mr. Radio measured **spoken notifications** —
the channel that goes to Rick, down a different code path, which the tutor never touches.

**The second channel was not a bystander.** Rick had been asking for shorter notifications since the
TTS acts of part 1 — the spoken cap, the slider, the three-sentence rule in the turn rider. That ask
was live the whole time and it was not working: notifications kept arriving long enough to be
punishing to sit through when read aloud. The instruction existed. Compliance did not.

So this is not an effect that landed in the wrong place. **It is one correction producing two.** The
tutor gave the model a worked example of what "shorter" actually looks like on a message it had
written itself, and the model carried the lesson to the other channel where it had already been asked
for the same thing and had not managed to deliver it. A rule the fleet had been nodding along to for
weeks started being followed, in a code path the fix never touched.

Equal 8-day windows either side of the cutover, authored notifications only:

| metric | before | after | delta |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| median chars | 272 | 287 | +6% |
| **p90 chars** | **1009** | **738** | **−27%** |
| mean chars | 380 | 345 | −9% |
| n | 1,914 | 4,923 | |

**The distribution did not shift down. Its right tail was cut.** The typical notification did not
shrink — it grew slightly. The mean fell only because the tail fell, and a mean that is being dragged
by its tail is not reporting a change in the middle of the distribution.

It replicates inside each project separately, which is what makes it stateable at all:

| project | p90 before → after | p50 before → after |
|---|---|---|
| lupin | 1109 → 802 | 264 → 290 |
| plan | 905 → 554 | 271 → 282 |

**Rick's sense of it was half right, and right about the half he would notice.** A 1,000-character
notification read aloud is the one that registers as too long. A 272-character one is not what
anybody complains about. What changed is that the notifications experienced as bloated stopped
arriving, and the ordinary ones were left alone — which is, precisely, what the mandate asked for
and what no rule in Acts 0 through 14 had managed to produce in this channel.

### Two rival explanations, killed with receipts rather than argument

**The 500-character spoken cap.** `lupin-app.ini` line 1073 sets `cosa voice spoken char cap = 500`;
line 1078 sets `cosa voice enforce spoken char cap = False`. Configured, not enforced — and the data
confirms it directly: **808 post-cutover rows exceed 500 characters.** If clipping were producing the
tail cut, those rows could not exist.

**The turn rider's "≤3 sentences AND ≤60 words."** That is a real length pressure on composed output,
and it landed **2026-08-03** — ten days before the cutover, sitting entirely inside the BEFORE window.
A constant already in force throughout the before period cannot produce a step at 08-13. If anything
its presence makes the tail cut harder to produce, not easier.

### The measurement problem was bigger than the effect

This is the reusable part, and it belongs in this document more than the number does. **The effect is
a 27% tail move. Each error that had to be cleared before that number could be read was larger than
the effect itself, and each one produced a confident-looking wrong answer.**

**First cut: 416 rows out of 317,509.** The filter was `sender_persona IS NOT NULL` — principled-looking,
since a persona means a real session composed it. But `sender_persona` is populated almost only on DMs.
The filter selected the one channel the study is explicitly *not* about and discarded the channel it is.

**Second cut: a median of exactly 75 characters on both sides.** Identical to the digit across the
cutover. That is the tell — a metric that does not move at all, on a table where everything moves, is
usually measuring something the process under study does not generate. It was not: **52,612 of 75,673
rows** were machine strings, led by `Done: mcp__cosa-voice___dm_send_fn` at 10,038 and
`Done: mcp__cosa-voice__notify` at 4,315.

The filter that finally worked names the usual exclusions and then adds one clause that does the real
work:

> **the exact message text occurs ≤ 2 times table-wide**

**A string that recurs is a template, not authorship.** Everything else on the list removes machine
strings somebody already thought to name; this clause removes the ones nobody thought of, *including
the ones that had not been written yet when the filter was designed.* It is the only part that
generalizes. And the threshold is 2 rather than 1 on purpose: a genuinely authored sentence
occasionally repeats by coincidence, and dropping exact duplicates would bias against short messages,
which collide more often.

The same trap caught the same analyst again, one week later and three orders of magnitude larger. A
follow-up cut splitting questions from statements was first run *without* the filter, over all 293,410
non-DM rows, and reported the finding **backwards** — questions 114 chars, statements 43 — because the
unfiltered population is dominated by short hook pings that are almost never questions. Same table,
same query logic, opposite conclusion, and **the wrong version looked perfectly plausible.**

> The filter is not a detail of the method. It is the method.

### What the document refuses to claim, stated by its own author

**Causation is not established.** The tutor shipped fleet-wide in a single cutover — every session that
could have been measured received the treatment on the same day at the same sha. That makes this an
uncontrolled before/after, and an uncontrolled before/after cannot separate the treatment from anything
else that changed that day. The spillover story fits the data; so would any other fleet-wide change on
2026-08-13.

A difference-in-differences would settle it: find a project the tutor never touched, measure the same
windows, and check whether the tail moved in the treated projects *relative to* the untreated one.
**He looked for such a project and did not find one.** So the honest sentence is the one he wrote:

> Notification length changed shape at the tutor cutover, in the direction the spillover hypothesis
> predicts. The tutor is a candidate cause. It is not a demonstrated one.

There is a second caveat he volunteered rather than waited to be asked. Daily p50 ranges from **106 to
431 characters** across the window — a spread several times the size of the before/after difference in
the median — and daily p50 tracks session count, which moved from **2 to 81** over the same period. A
composition shift that size can move aggregate statistics on its own, with no per-session behavior
change at all. The equal-window design and the per-project replication are not decoration; they are the
reason the tail result survives that objection. **And they still do not make it causal.**

### What it bought, in the only unit that matters

Part 1 opened on a number: **four of every five tokens the fleet spent were going to talking about the
work rather than doing it.** One in five reached the actual job. That number is why this whole story
exists — not the aesthetics of a long DM, but the fact that the overhead had eaten the budget.

Rick's reading after the tutor:

| | before the quest | after Act 15 |
|---|---:|---:|
| tokens spent on communications overhead | **~80%** — four in five | **~30%** — three in ten |
| tokens reaching the work | **~20%** — one in five | **~70%** — seven in ten |

**The work's share of the budget went from one fifth to seven tenths.** That is not a trim. It is
close to a quadrupling of how much of every token actually lands on the job.

And the last stretch of it was free. The tutor was built to shorten DMs and it shortened DMs; the
notification channel came along without a second system, a second model call, or a second line of
code. **One behavioral correction, paid for once, collected twice.**

### Why this is the ending and not merely the latest news

Act 14 ended on a sentence about the limits of measurement: *a measurement with one arm cannot check
itself.* Act 15 answers it from a direction the trial was not designed to look. The two-arm trial
watched the treated channel. The result that arrived came from **an untreated one, moving the same way**
— which is the one shape a retry artifact cannot take, because there are no retries in a channel with
no refusals in it.

That is a stronger form of evidence than the trial was built to produce, and a weaker form of proof
than anybody wanted. **Both are true, and the document says both.**

**The real lesson is about what a behavioral correction is.** Every act before this one treated the
problem as a rule to be written, an instrument to be built, or a gate to be closed — each one aimed at
a specific channel, and each one only ever fixing the channel it was aimed at. The tutor is the first
control that changed how the model writes rather than what it is permitted to send, and a change in
how the model writes does not stay in its lane. **Correct a behavior and you get the behavior
everywhere it applies; block an output and you get compliance exactly where you blocked it.**

That is worth more than the 27%. It says the fleet's register is reachable at runtime — not by adding
another paragraph to a mandate the sessions already agree with, but by showing the model its own
sentence, rewritten.

The through-line holds to the last act. Every correction in this story moved discretion away from
whoever was being graded — the model, then the fleet, then the experimenters. Act 15 moves it one step
further, onto the person reporting the win: the author had a result Rick would have been glad to hear
stated plainly, and wrote *"candidate cause, not a demonstrated one"* instead.

## What the protocol actually is, in one table

| Rule | Says | Fires on |
|---|---|---|
| **KISS** | Keep It Short/Sweet | the disease itself |
| **Say 3LoL** | Three lines or less — headline + two supporting sentences | any reply |
| **NoMC C2C** | No meta conversation, cut to the chase | preambles, narration, restatement |
| **NoAA** | No aphorisms or apologies | **being corrected** |
| **NoDrama** | State the defect, the fix, the receipt | **writing up a finding** |
| **WaHH** | We're All Humans Here — plain English, no invented vocabulary | **writing to a peer** |

**The first five govern length and posture. WaHH governs register**, and it is the only rule that
overrides another one: jargon *is* compression, so when WaHH and KISS disagree, **WaHH wins.**

| Glyph | Direction | Means | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 😘 | operator → session | fire the entire mandate | 🫡, then tightened output |
| 🏆 | operator → session | that was right, do more of it | 🫡, then keep working |
| 📷 | operator → session | document and checkpoint your work | 🫡, then the checkpoint + sha |
| 🫡 | session → operator | received, complying | *nothing else is sent* |

**Detail is not banned — it is routed.** Rich content goes to the notification `abstract` card, to
`src/rnd/`, to post-game retros, and to the task store. Tables and code blocks are **content, not
prose**, and never count against the line budget. What is capped is the narration *around* the
content.

### And what the instrument is, in one table

| Instrument | Measures | How | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DM Style Contract** | nothing — it *instructs* | spliced into the MCP instructions payload, no toggle | live, always on |
| **Length** | word count → 5 buckets, plus an `overage` ratio that keeps moving after the grade saturates | pure Python, no model | live |
| **Directness** | where the verdict sits, and what trails it | v1 asks the model to grade; **v2 asks it to *locate*, and Python scores the position** | v2 shipped |
| **Tone** | plain-spoken colleague, or invented vocabulary | fixed-rubric model call | live |
| **Rejection at 150 words** | — | refuse an over-long DM outright; the refusal names the action, never the threshold | **live — the trial arm, 14 of 28 hour-blocks** |
| **Rewriter** (the **DM tutor**) | — | compress and de-jargon an over-long DM — local Phi-4, temperature 0.0, zero marginal cost | **live fleet-wide since 2026-08-13, sha `b8d10bd3`** — see Act 15 |

**A grade is advisory, and that is its limit.** The judge published 👎s all evening and blocked
nobody — which is precisely how a 1000-word DM still went out. The lever above it is refusal.

## The architecture underneath: progressive disclosure

*Detail is not banned, it is routed* appears in every act of this story. It has a name, and the name
is worth stating because it is the same pattern the tooling already uses in the other direction.

**Progressive disclosure** — information released in layers, each one opened only if the layer before
it was not enough.

| Direction | Layers |
|---|---|
| **To the LLM** (Agent Skills) | frontmatter metadata → `SKILL.md` instructions → reference files, loaded on demand |
| **To the operator** (notifications) | spoken headline → notification bubble → abstract card → doc link |

The parallel is exact. A Skill does not push its reference files into context on the chance they are
needed; a notification does not speak its inventory on the chance the listener wants it. Both put a
cheap, complete-in-itself summary in front of the reader, and both make the next layer **one step
away** rather than **already delivered**.

This is why the two-channel design survived the mandate untouched while the prose inside it was cut
by more than half. The channels were never the problem — a spoken verdict and a written record are
complementary, not duplicative. What was wrong was that **every layer was being filled to the depth of
the deepest one**: the spoken line carrying the card's inventory, the card carrying the design doc's
reasoning, the history entry carrying the retro's narrative. A disclosure architecture in which every
layer says everything is not progressive; it is the same document, printed four times, in four places
that each charge for it.

**The mandate is the discipline that makes the architecture work as designed.** Caps on the spoken
payload, caps on written artifacts, the routing rule that sends detail to `abstract` and `src/rnd/`
and the post-game corpus — each one is an instruction to *stop filling the shallow layer with the deep
layer's content*. The slider is the same idea implemented in hardware: proof that when the top layer
is disciplined, a quarter of it is more useful than an eighth of an undisciplined one.

## The through-line

Read as a sequence, every correction Rick made moved discretion **away from the model and toward the
reader**, and made the correction mechanism **cheaper**:

- *"only when ASKED"* — the author may not certify its own exception.
- Four triggers pruned — a false fire costs more than a miss, because a broadcast cannot be un-sent.
- 🫡 replaces a prohibition — ban a reply without offering an alternative and you get the reply anyway.
- 😘 replaces a paragraph — a reminder more expensive than the disease will not be used.
- Written artifacts un-exempted — the surface the model writes is the surface it will exempt.
- NoAA — the model's proof that it learned is itself a cost the user pays.
- NoDrama — a finding that leans on its stakes has to be re-verified when the stakes shrink.
- WaHH — a term the writer coined is a cost the reader pays, and one the operator cannot audit.

None of these are rules about taste. Each one closes a path by which a verbose model, acting in good
faith, talks its way back to verbosity.

**Act 11 extends the through-line in a direction the first ten did not reach.** Every earlier
correction moved discretion from the model to the reader on the question *how much*. WaHH moves it on
the question *for whom* — and it is the only one that had to override an existing rule to do it,
because the model's compression instinct and the operator's readability are genuinely opposed. The
first five rules assumed that a shorter message was a better one. The sixth is the case where it
isn't.

**Acts 12 through 14 change the kind of answer, not the direction of it.** Every correction through
Act 11 was a better *rule*. Act 12 is the point where rules ran out: the two worst messages in the
corpus both **end with the rule they are breaking**, so the next move could only be an instrument
that reads the channel rather than a sentence that asks it nicely. That is the same lesson as 🫡 and
😘 — a control has to *do* something, not *say* something — arriving at the last surface where
nobody had applied it.

**Act 14 is where measurement ran out, and it is the same move one level up.** The instrument
graded 776 messages and the fleet went on writing at 91 words against a 60-word target. That
looks like a verdict and it is not one: every message sat in a single condition, so *the grades
don't work* and *this setup cannot see whether they work* were the same observation wearing
different clothes. Rules gave way to an instrument because a sentence cannot check anyone; the
instrument gave way to a trial because **a measurement with one arm cannot check itself.**

> Every correction in this document moved discretion away from whoever was being graded. The
> last one moves it away from the people running the experiment: **the arms were assigned, the
> measures were named, and the hours were randomized before anybody looked at a number.**

**And Act 15 moves it one step past that, onto the person reporting the result.** The rewriter shipped,
the tail of an untreated channel fell 27%, and the author wrote *"a candidate cause, not a demonstrated
one"* — declining the win he had every incentive to claim. That is the same instinct as every act before
it, arriving at the last surface where it could still be applied: **the report itself.**

The arc, in one line: *a rule asked, a grade watched, a refusal blocked, and a rewriter simply did the
work* — and the thing that finally moved was in a channel none of them were aimed at.

## Coda — prescribe, proscribe, or model

Read the ladder again with different verbs on it.

| Act | The move | The verb |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | a sixth rule, written into the mandate | **prescribe** — tell them what to do |
| **12** | an instrument that grades every message | **measure** — tell them how they did |
| **14** | a refusal at 150 words | **proscribe** — forbid the thing outright |
| **15** | a rewriter that compresses and sends | **model** — show them what it looks like |

The first three are the tools any organization reaches for, and every one of them fixed exactly the
channel it was pointed at and nothing else. The fourth spilled. **A rule is a boundary and stays where
you drew it; an example is a demonstration and travels wherever the behavior applies.**

That is not a fact about language models in particular. It is the oldest finding in how groups
actually change: people do what is demonstrated around them far more reliably than what is posted on
the wall. The interesting part is that a fleet of agents turns out to be subject to it too — and that
the demonstration did not even have to come from a peer. It came from the fleet's own messages, handed
back rewritten.

**This is where the frontier is, and every vendor shipping agent fleets is about to arrive at it.** The
coordination problem in a multi-agent system is usually posed as a routing question — who talks to
whom, through what protocol, with what schema. It is at least as much a **group-dynamics** question:
what register the members adopt, how that register spreads, and what it costs the humans standing
downstream of it. Acts 0 through 14 are a long record of trying to solve that with policy. Act 15 is
the first time it moved, and it moved because somebody changed what the fleet *saw* rather than what
it was *told*.

Rick's own reaction to the result was that it sounded like the science fiction he grew up on. Not the
part where the machines do something nobody asked for — the part where a system large enough to have a
culture turns out to be steerable by the same unglamorous lever that steers every other culture:
**not the rule, the example.**

### A conjecture, labelled as one — why any of this happened at all

Everything above describes *what* the fleet did and *what* moved it. Rick has a claim about **why the
problem existed in the first place**, and it is worth writing down precisely because it is not
established. It is a hypothesis with a testable shape, and it is his:

> These models were trained to talk to a human, turn by turn. Put them in a room with each other and
> take the human out, and the behaviour that made them good at the first job is exactly the one that
> degrades in the second.

The argument runs like this. **Mirroring is not a defect the fleet developed; it is a competence the
models were trained to have.** Matching a user's register — their vocabulary, their density, their
formality — is most of what makes a turn-by-turn assistant feel like it is listening. That skill is
aimed at a human on the other side, and it is calibrated by one: a person who does not understand a
message says so, and the register corrects on the next turn.

Now remove the human. The skill does not switch off — it has nothing to switch off *for*. Each session
still matches the register of what it just received, except what it just received was written by
another session doing the same thing. **The calibration loop is gone and the matching loop is not**,
so the register walks, and it walks in a direction nobody chose. That is a single mechanism producing
both halves of Act 13 at once: the register is the model's own (half one) *and* the channel has no
reader who pushes back (half two), because the reader who used to push back was the very thing the
architecture removed.

It also explains a detail that otherwise needs its own excuse — why the drift is **shared** rather than
per-seat. Parallel drift would give every session its own dialect. A trained-in matching reflex, fed
peer output, gives them all the *same* one.

**What would make this more than a good story.** Act 13 already names the query and it has still never
been run: average words per DM against hours-since-the-last-human-message, across a long unattended
run. This conjecture makes a sharper prediction than "register degrades over time" — it says the curve
should key on **time since a human spoke**, not on elapsed time, message count, or session age. If a
fleet running twelve hours with a human checking in hourly stays flat while one running six hours with
nobody watching walks, the mechanism is contact, not duration. **That is a distinguishing test, and
the corpus cannot answer it today** — the rows carry sender, recipient, project, session and
timestamp, and nothing marking when a human last spoke. One field stamped at write time, and the
conjecture becomes a query.

**Why it belongs in this document rather than in a footnote.** If it is right, the coordination
problem every agent-fleet vendor is walking toward is not primarily about protocols. It is that the
social reflexes these models were trained to aim at people get aimed at each other the moment you
build a fleet, with nothing on the receiving end to correct them — and the fix is not a better schema.
It is putting something in the loop that behaves the way the missing human did. **The DM tutor is a
crude first version of exactly that: a reader that pushes back.**

That is the claim. It is one operator's read of one fleet, it has a named falsifier, and the field
work behind it was done at a kitchen-table scale by somebody who mostly wanted his notifications to
stop being so long.

**Acts 12 through 14 carry claims of three different strengths, and each is flagged where it
appears.** Act 14's baseline is the strongest thing here — **776 graded messages**, one row per
message on disk, median 91 words against a 60-word target and 78% over it. Its weakness is
stated in the act itself and is structural rather than accidental: **every one of those 776 sat
in a single condition**, which is the reason a two-arm trial exists at all.

Act 13's second half is **an operator observation, not a measurement**: the
drift-with-time-since-human-contact claim has a named test and nobody has run it, and the
mirroring claim has a second named test that needs nothing built. The paired readings in Act 12
are **n=1 per seat**, self-reported by the graded parties; they are kept because they fail in
*opposite* directions, which is harder to produce by accident than agreement would be.

**And Act 14 has no result yet.** It is a design and a running window, reported here before its
data is in — deliberately, because a design published after its outcome is known is a design
nobody can check.
