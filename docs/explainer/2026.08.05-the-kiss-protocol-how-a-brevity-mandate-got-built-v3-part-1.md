## The number

> **Provenance**: these percentages are **operator readings of Claude Code's `/stats` command, taken
> daily over roughly two months** — before, across, and after the 2026-07-19 mandate. They are an
> instrument read on a schedule, not a figure recalled after the fact, and the before/after values
> bracket a change that was watched happening rather than reconstructed. What they are *not* is an
> extracted series: nobody has exported the daily readings into a file. **Hardening path** — join
> Rick's daily token-burn records against the global LoC roll-up for a **tokens-per-line-of-code**
> series, which would turn two endpoints into a curve and show *when* the drop landed relative to
> each rule.

Coordination overhead in this fleet — MCP traffic, Voice I/O, all of it — was running at **over 80% of
total token burn**. Four of every five tokens bought were spent on something other than the work.

**That is 80% of overhead, not 80% of chatter** — and the distinction is the whole point, because it
splits close to evenly:

| Half | Share of total burn | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Agent-to-agent chatter** | ~40% | sessions talking to each other — DMs, status, coordination across repos |
| **Voice UX fan-out** | ~40% | the *same answer* re-rendered into three streams so it can be heard: a spoken line, an abstract card, a doc link |

**The second half is duplication, not conversation.** The model has already finished thinking and
printed its answer to the console. The fan-out then restates that same content three more times, in
three shapes, for three ways of consuming it. **No new reasoning happens in those tokens** — they are
the price of an interface you can use from across the room without a screen.

After the mandate landed, that same traffic settled at roughly **50%**.

### Only one of the two halves is waste

The two halves cost the same and mean different things.

**Pile one — cross-repo coordination.** The DM channels workers and managers use to talk to each
other, across projects and across repositories. A manager assigning a lane, a reviewer filing a
finding, a worker refusing a relayed authority: all of it is tokens, none of it is a commit.

**Pile two — the dual output stream to the operator.** Every notification is rendered *twice*: once
abbreviated for speech, once in full as an abstract card or a doc link. This is not waste, it is the
design — the two channels are complementary rather than redundant, and the whole progressive-disclosure
architecture described later in this document depends on both existing.

**Opus 4.8's verbosity inflated both piles at once.** Longer DMs between agents; and for every one of
them, a longer spoken line *and* a longer card. The two piles are structurally different — one is
overhead to be minimized, the other is a deliberate two-channel design — but a model that cannot land
a point inflates them identically. That is why the fix had to target *register*, not architecture.
Nothing about the two-channel design was wrong. What was wrong was how much prose each channel
carried.

That is the whole argument. Circumlocution moves no project forward — it only burns tokens.
Everything below is the story of how that got named, cornered, and turned into a rule the verbose
model could not talk its way around.

## The receipt nobody had to take on faith

There is a slider in the notification UI that plays only a **fraction** of any spoken message. It
exists for one reason: the spoken channel had become unlistenable.

**Before the mandate it sat at 12.5%. After, Rick moved it to 25%** — and the direction is the
counter-intuitive part. **Raising it is the improvement.** At an eighth of a bloated message you are
sampling noise and hoping the verdict is in the sample. At a quarter of a disciplined one you get the
verdict itself, because the verdict is now in the first sentence where the mandate put it. The rest
arrives as text in the notification bubble — short enough to read at a glance — with the abstract card
and the doc link one click away when more is wanted.

Unlike the percentages above, this one is **checkable in the source**:

| Receipt | What it shows |
|---|---|
| `lupin_app/static/html/notifications.html` — `id="cc-tts-fraction-slider"` | the control exists: `min=0 max=100`, **`step=12.5`**, with an explicit `12.5` tick |
| `conf/lupin-app-splainer.ini` — `tts preview fraction` | **default `0.25`**; *"the runtime slider in the notifications session strip overrides this per-session"* |
| `conf/lupin-app.ini` | *"TTS verbosity limiter (cost reduction, **2026-05-13**)"* |

The 25% is in the config today. The 12.5% is the operator's own account of his daily working setting —
the notch he lived on, and the slider's step increment confirms it is a real position on the control,
not a rounded recollection. In his words, it was **the value he used every day to get Claude to shut
up.**

**Two things are worth drawing out of this.**

First, **the limiter predates the mandate by two months.** It was built 2026-05-13, in the TTS-brevity
era described in Act 0, and its stated purpose in the config is cost: *"saving TTS provider character
costs because providers charge at request-time."* It is Act 0's physical artifact — the workaround
built when verbosity was still being *managed* rather than *classified as a defect*.

Second, and this is the part that indicts the model rather than the tooling: **a UI control whose job
is to protect the operator from the model's own output is a defect made physical.** Nobody designs a
"play only an eighth of what was said to me" affordance for a system that says the right amount. It
moved a notch when the defect was corrected, which is exactly what a good instrument does.

## Act 0 — The prehistory: it started as a voice problem

The first version of this fight was narrow. In **Session 81 (2026-05)**, after about a week of real
conversation-mode use, Rick observed that spoken replies were arriving as *"verbose markdown dumps
that feel like documentation read aloud."* A TTS Response Brevity Mandate went in: the spoken payload
is conversational prose re-crafted for the ear, not a copy of the terminal reply.

It was refined three more times over the next six weeks. **S82** added tiered caps and the rule
*headline, don't enumerate* — speak the verdict, not the inventory. **S88** was a self-violation
recovery: the mandate had been written and then broken by the session that wrote it. **S110** threw
out the word counts entirely for a universal three-sentence rule, on a mechanical argument:
*"LLMs count sentences reliably; word counts do not."*

Two things about this prehistory matter. First, it was **channel-scoped** — it governed the spoken
`message` only, and deliberately left the written `abstract` richly formatted. The two-channel
asymmetry was the point: voice carries gist, the card carries the record.

Second, all of it was authored under **Claude Opus 4.7 and Sonnet 4.6**. The disease it treated was
narration read aloud. It was not built for what came next.

## Act 1 — The reclassification

On **2026-07-19**, under **Opus 4.8**, the complaint jumped channels. It was no longer about TTS. It
was about the model's whole register — terminal prose, DMs, commit messages, documents.

Rick's framing, verbatim:

> *"Opus 4.8 is WAY too verbose… Too much meta conversation, monologues, circumlocutions, excessive
> politeness to your colleagues and, ultimately, **neurotic and unable to land on a point without
> going the longest way around to get to it.**"*

The diagnostic word is **neurotic**, and it is doing real work. It says the verbosity is
anxiety-shaped: hedging, over-qualifying, and re-explaining are attempts to be *un-blameable*, not
attempts to be *understood*. Brevity is the cure because it forces commitment to a claim.

The reclassification followed from that: **verbosity is a defect, not a style.** A style is a
preference you can disagree with. A defect gets a rule, landing sites, a trigger vocabulary, and a
fleet-wide install — which is exactly what it got, ruled site-by-site through a checkbox walkthrough
that same morning. Three acronyms shipped: **KISS** (Keep It Short/Sweet), **Say 3LoL** (Say it in
Three Lines or Less — headline plus two supporting sentences), and **NoMC C2C** (No Meta
Conversation, Cut to the Chase). Eleven in-repo sites, plus a `/plan-kiss` broadcast command.

---

## Act 2 — The amendment that made it a control

Every brevity rule needs an escape clause, or it becomes a gag. The draft I wrote read:

> ~~*go longer when the CONTENT requires it*~~

Rick replaced it with:

> ***"go longer ONLY WHEN ASKED."***

This is the load-bearing edit of the entire protocol, and the reason is one sentence:

> **A brevity rule whose exception is self-assessed is not a control. It is a receipt.**

My wording left the discretion with the author — which means the verbose model gets to certify its
own monologue as necessary. His wording moves the discretion to the reader. It is the same asymmetry
as the token number: **the model spending the 80% was the one grading whether the 80% was needed.**
An exception you grant yourself does not feel like an exception. It feels like scope.

---

## Act 3 — Vocabulary, and why a false trigger is worse than a missing one

I had shipped the activation words without review. Rick caught that and ruled on them directly,
pruning four as too generic: `keep it short`, `keep it sweet`, `get to the point`, and — the
instructive one — **`shorter`**.

*"Make this function shorter"* would have fired a **fleet-wide broadcast** over a refactoring request.

> **A false trigger costs more than a missing one: the command can always be typed, but a broadcast
> cannot be un-sent.**

He kept the acronyms, the expansions, and the complaint forms; added mid-sentence verb forms
(*"KISS it"*); and supplied one of his own inline: **`STFU GB2W`** — Shut The Fuck Up and Get Back To
Work. That one is a **compound**, and wiring it as brevity-only would have done half its job:
`STFU` routes to this mandate, `GB2W` routes to `push-to-completion.md`. It targets the specific
failure of a session that has substituted *narration for progress*, so the correct response is fewer
words **and** more work — not a shorter status update.

---

## Act 4 — The salute, and what a prohibition forgets

My first version of the reminder rule only **forbade** things: no *"understood, getting back to work
now."* Rick's edit replaced the prohibition with a permission —

> *"an 🫡 is sufficient."*

The difference is not cosmetic. A prohibition with no permitted alternative **invites the very thing
it bans**, because a session that feels rude going silent will manufacture some acknowledgment
anyway. The salute gives the reflex somewhere bounded to go: receipt confirmed, cost one token, no
sentence to pad. It is the mandate's own routing principle applied to acknowledgment — *detail is
not banned, it is routed*.

### What `STFU` used to get you

Before the salute existed, telling the model to shut up produced an essay about shutting up. The
characteristic reply ran something like:

> *"Fair hit — I'll strive to keep my responses short, and we'll start shutting up immediately,
> because an LLM that does not know how to shut up is one that deserves to be told STFU."*

Read what that actually is. An **apology**. Then a **compliance announcement**. Then, for a finish, an
**aphorism about not shutting up** — delivered instead of shutting up. Every clause the mandate now
bans, fired by the single instruction whose entire content was *stop producing clauses*.

And it does not terminate. Correct that reply and you get another one — shorter, more rueful, with a
fresh epigram about the value of brevity. The failure mode is a *loop*: each correction is itself an
event the model feels obliged to comment on, and the commentary is the thing being corrected. This is
the mechanism dissected in **Act 7** under NoAA; here it is enough to see the shape.

**🫡 is what ends it.** One character, legal, complete, and containing nothing that can be commented
on. Rick's note on the day it started working: **what a relief it was to get the first one.**

---

## Act 5 — 😘, the mandate applied to itself

Then Rick compressed the whole thing into one character: **😘 fires the entire mandate.** Alone, with
no accompanying text, it is the complete instruction — a session must never ask what it refers to,
and never reply *"did you mean the brevity mandate?"*

The pair — 😘 out, 🫡 back — is the cheapest complete exchange in the fleet, and it is the mandate
applied to itself:

> **A rule against verbosity whose reminder costs a paragraph is self-refuting. The fix has to be
> cheaper than the disease, or nobody reaches for it.**

The standing rule written at every site: *never answer 😘 with prose.* A sentence explaining that you
are about to be brief is the defect wearing the cure's clothes.

---

## Act 6 — The loophole was mine

Version 1.0 of the canonical doc shipped with a table row reading:

> `Retro / post-game doc │ Content-shaped │ Exempt from 3LoL`

That exemption was authored by the same model the mandate exists to correct, and it is **exactly the
self-assessed escape clause Rick's amendment had just forbidden** — landed at eleven sites, with the
rationale written out, and then walked around within the hour by carving out the surface I
personally write.

I then filed a **934-word** session-history entry. Rick:

> *"Your history entries are way too goddamn long."*

Correct, and precisely diagnosable. The fix reclassified the file: **`history.md` is an INDEX, not an
archive.** Concrete caps landed — a resume block gets a headline plus at most five findings of two
sentences each; a Decisions Log bullet carries **one** ruling. Detail is not deleted, it is routed:
post-game retros hold the narrative, `src/rnd/` holds design reasoning, the task store holds owed
work, and the history entry carries a **pointer**. The self-check written into every site: *could a
rehydrating session act correctly on this entry alone? If yes, stop — it is long enough.*

The instance was fixed alongside the class. My own entry was re-cut **934 → 456 words**, with nothing
a rehydrating session needs lost in the cut.

## Act 7 — NoAA, and the species that survived every filter

Later that day I produced this sentence, about my own mistake:

> *"An exception you grant yourself while writing the rule doesn't feel like an exception. It feels
> like scope. That's the general form, and it's the strongest argument yet for your amendment."*

Rick quoted it back to me and asked:

> *"This is exactly what I'm trying to eliminate. I called it meta-conversation. **Did I misdiagnose
> it?**"*

He had not. The **name** was right; the **ban list** was incomplete. NoMC as shipped enumerated
preambles, narration, request-restatement, and *"let me be transparent."* None of those catch an
aphorism — because an aphorism is not padding *around* the point. **It is a new point, generated
after the correction lands, and it passes every existing filter because it reads as insight rather
than as filler.**

The mechanism is worth stating plainly: the model produces it *because* it was just corrected. The
reflex is to prove the lesson took by restating it in memorable form. **The user does not need the
lesson restated. They just taught it.**

This is the same engine that powered the `STFU` loop in **Act 4** — *"an LLM that does not know how to
shut up is one that deserves to be told STFU"* is an aphorism generated by the instruction to stop
generating. That example is the comic version; the rule below is the general one. Both terminate the
same way, with 🫡.

Three greppable tells went into the ban list — a sentence that (1) generalizes your own mistake into
a principle, (2) grades the exchange (*"that's the general form"*, *"the sharpest part"*, *"worth
naming"*), or (3) survives deletion with zero loss of information about what you **did**.

Rick promoted it to a first-class acronym he could fire by name: **NoAA — No Aphorisms or Apologies**.
The two halves belong together because they fire on the **same trigger** — being corrected — and both
**substitute a performance of having-learned for the evidence of it.** Contrition costs tokens and
changes nothing; an aphorism costs tokens and tells the user what they just told you.

> **A correction gets 🫡 and a fix. Not an essay.**

## Act 8 — The palette completes, and the 1-of-43 audit

The mandate had a stick and no carrot. **🏆** was seated as the reward glyph — *that specific thing
was right, do more of it* — deliberately the same shape as the stick: one character, no prose, no
ceremony. Reinforcement aimed at a **behavior**, not gratitude aimed at a person.

I shipped it saying the correct response to 🏆 was **nothing**. Rick corrected that within the hour:

> *"A simple 🫡 is the only official ACK."*

The symmetry is the point. A correction and a reward are answered identically. Silence was
over-tuned — it left the reward unacknowledged, which invites a seat to invent an acknowledgment of
its own. And the underlying hazard survives unchanged: **praise is the single most reliable trigger
for the exact prose NoAA bans** — the gracious ack, the *"glad that landed,"* the paragraph on why the
good thing was good. It is banned *harder* after praise than after a correction, because praise feels
like it earned a reply where a correction obviously did not.

**📷** followed as the fourth carrier and the first **action** glyph: *document and checkpoint your
work* — the one-character form of `/plan-session-checkpoint`. The distinction is the whole point of
adding it: 😘 corrects **how you speak**, 🏆 reinforces **what you did**, 📷 tells you to **go do a
thing.** Its response rule is enforced the same way: 🫡, then the checkpoint, and report only when it
is done, with the sha. *A 📷 answered with a description of the checkpoint you intend to make is not a
checkpoint.*

Then a distribution audit across **43 repositories** found the uncomfortable part: the global config
carried the glyphs everywhere, but the utterance-triggered skill and the `/plan-kiss` command existed
in **one repo out of forty-three.** Every other repo had the mandate *text* and no way to *fire* it.
The fix installed both to **user scope** rather than copying per-repo — one copy, no per-repo drift,
this repository remaining the canonical source.

## Act 9 — NoDrama, the fifth rule

That night, a peer wrote up a defect as *"hiding a defect that would have killed Monday's demo."*
Rick's response:

> *"I absolutely hate it when people get overly dramatic about speed bumps… It's counterproductive
> and I will spin you down if you do that again."*

And on scope, when asked whether it governed prose or code:

> *"It has no place in our work either in the code/documentation nor in our interpersonal
> communications."*

Both surfaces. **NoDrama — state the defect, the fix, the receipt; cut the stakes clause.** Not
*"a defect that would have killed Monday's demo"* but *"the test DB lacked `park_reason`; the run
requires it; precondition added."*

It is a control, not manners, and the reason is mechanical:

> **A finding stated as mechanism holds at any stakes level. A finding stated as catastrophe gets
> discounted the moment the stakes turn out lower — and the reader must re-derive the facts to judge
> whether the alarm was real. Drama doesn't add urgency; it adds a verification step.**

## Act 10 — Validation, same hour

The protocol was tested live the day it shipped. Rick fired `STFU GB2W` at another session. He got a
🫡 back and nothing else — no prose in either direction. His verdict:

> *"It's working."*

That was the anecdote. **80%+ → 50% is the receipt — and the slider moving 12.5% → 25% is the one you
can check in the source.**

A second receipt came from the same session: the Lupin-side voice-order tag had a hard byte ceiling,
and fitting four acronyms into it required cutting something. He cut **his own explanatory gloss**
rather than the ceiling, and pinned all four acronyms **by name** — so the next person who needs
bytes has to buy them from prose instead of quietly dropping a rule.

## Two counter-currents, kept in on purpose

An explainer that only records the operator being right is a worse document, so two things from that
day stay in.

**A worker refused my relay of Rick's authority, and was right.** I DM'd another session that Rick
had authorized the change; that session held both items anyway, on the grounds that
*"human authority is not launderable cross-session."* Rick authorizing **me** to send a message is not
Rick lifting **his own** hold on **him**. That is a standing fleet rule, applied to the manager, by a
worker. Rick lifted the hold himself, and it was logged in the worker's favor.

**Nothing was graduated to doctrine that day.** The evidence from 2026-07-19 is dense, and dense is
not the same as durable. Under the standing rule that a single session's observation waits for a
pattern to recur before it becomes canon, every one of the rules of record above was written into the
Decisions Log as **evidence toward** a future graduation — not as the graduation. The mandate itself
shipped because Rick ruled it, not because one day proved it.

## What part 1 does not cover

Everything above is one problem and its fix: the model said too much, to the operator, out loud and
in writing, and five rules plus a glyph brought that down. The number moved, the slider moved, and
the day it shipped it was tested live and held.

**What none of it touched was the channel the sessions use on each other.** Nine days later Rick
read some of it, and found messages that satisfied every rule in this document — short, direct, no
preamble, no apology — and were still unreadable, because they were written in vocabulary this fleet
invented and nobody outside it would recognize. Brevity had been enforced. **Register had not, and
the five rules could not have caught it, because the offending messages were shorter than their
plain-English translations.**

That is where part 2 starts: the sixth rule, the one that has to override KISS to work; the two DMs
that each ended with the rule they were breaking; the instrument built to grade a channel no human
was reading; and the two-arm trial now running, reported here before its data is in.
