# The KISS Protocol — how a brevity mandate got built

**An explainer.** How one operator's complaint about an LLM's speaking style became a six-rule
control with a one-character trigger, installed fleet-wide, in a single day — what a second
complaint, nine days later, revealed about the channel nobody had been reading, and what happened
when somebody finally built an instrument to read it.

**Written**: 2026-07-25 · **Updated**: 2026-08-02 (Acts 12–13 — the DM judge, and what the channel
does to the speaker)
**Subject**: Sessions 81 → 156 (2026-05 → 2026-08-02)
**Canonical rule**: [`workflow/brevity-mandate.md`](../../workflow/brevity-mandate.md)
**Fleet reminder**: `/plan-kiss`

---

## The number

> **Provenance**: these percentages are **operator readings of Claude Code's `/stats` command, taken
> daily over roughly two months** — before, across, and after the 2026-07-19 mandate. They are an
> instrument read on a schedule, not a figure recalled after the fact, and the before/after values
> bracket a change that was watched happening rather than reconstructed. What they are *not* is an
> extracted series: nobody has exported the daily readings into a file. **Hardening path** — join
> Rick's daily token-burn records against the global LoC roll-up for a **tokens-per-line-of-code**
> series, which would turn two endpoints into a curve and show *when* the drop landed relative to
> each rule.

Coordination overhead in this fleet — MCP traffic, Voice I/O, all of it — was running at **over 80% of total
token burn**. Two of every five tokens bought were spent on agents talking *about* the work rather
than doing it. And an equal sum of tokens were spent on splitting Claude Code's into 3 TTS constrained streams: 
notifications (summaries), abstracts (details), and document links for deep dives.

After the mandate landed, that same traffic settled at roughly **50%**.

### That 80% was not one thing

It divided across two piles, and the distinction matters because only one of them is overhead in the
pejorative sense.

**Pile one — cross-repo coordination.** The DM channels workers and managers use to talk to each
other, across projects and across repositories. A manager assigning a lane, a reviewer filing a
finding, a worker refusing a relayed authority: all of it is tokens, none of it is a commit.

**Pile two — the dual output stream to the operator.** Every notification is rendered *twice*: once
abbreviated for speech, once in full as an abstract card or a doc link. This is not waste, it is the
design — the two channels are complementary rather than redundant, and the whole progressive-disclosure
architecture described later in this document depends on both existing.

**Opus 4.8's verbosity inflated both piles at once.** Longer DMs between agents; and for every one of
them, a longer spoken line *and* a longer card. The two piles are structurally different — one is
overhead to be minimized, the other is a deliberate two-channel design — but a model that cannot land
a point inflates them identically. That is why the fix had to target *register*, not architecture.
Nothing about the two-channel design was wrong. What was wrong was how much prose each channel
carried.

That is the whole argument. Circumlocution moves no project forward — it only burns tokens.
Everything below is the story of how that got named, cornered, and turned into a rule the verbose
model could not talk its way around.

## The receipt nobody had to take on faith

There is a slider in the notification UI that plays only a **fraction** of any spoken message. It
exists for one reason: the spoken channel had become unlistenable.

**Before the mandate it sat at 12.5%. After, Rick moved it to 25%** — and the direction is the
counter-intuitive part. **Raising it is the improvement.** At an eighth of a bloated message you are
sampling noise and hoping the verdict is in the sample. At a quarter of a disciplined one you get the
verdict itself, because the verdict is now in the first sentence where the mandate put it. The rest
arrives as text in the notification bubble — short enough to read at a glance — with the abstract card
and the doc link one click away when more is wanted.

Unlike the percentages above, this one is **checkable in the source**:

| Receipt | What it shows |
|---|---|
| `lupin_app/static/html/notifications.html` — `id="cc-tts-fraction-slider"` | the control exists: `min=0 max=100`, **`step=12.5`**, with an explicit `12.5` tick |
| `conf/lupin-app-splainer.ini` — `tts preview fraction` | **default `0.25`**; *"the runtime slider in the notifications session strip overrides this per-session"* |
| `conf/lupin-app.ini` | *"TTS verbosity limiter (cost reduction, **2026-05-13**)"* |

The 25% is in the config today. The 12.5% is the operator's own account of his daily working setting —
the notch he lived on, and the slider's step increment confirms it is a real position on the control,
not a rounded recollection. In his words, it was **the value he used every day to get Claude to shut
up.**

**Two things are worth drawing out of this.**

First, **the limiter predates the mandate by two months.** It was built 2026-05-13, in the TTS-brevity
era described in Act 0, and its stated purpose in the config is cost: *"saving TTS provider character
costs because providers charge at request-time."* It is Act 0's physical artifact — the workaround
built when verbosity was still being *managed* rather than *classified as a defect*.

Second, and this is the part that indicts the model rather than the tooling: **a UI control whose job
is to protect the operator from the model's own output is a defect made physical.** Nobody designs a
"play only an eighth of what was said to me" affordance for a system that says the right amount. It
moved a notch when the defect was corrected, which is exactly what a good instrument does.

## Act 0 — The prehistory: it started as a voice problem

The first version of this fight was narrow. In **Session 81 (2026-05)**, after about a week of real
conversation-mode use, Rick observed that spoken replies were arriving as *"verbose markdown dumps
that feel like documentation read aloud."* A TTS Response Brevity Mandate went in: the spoken payload
is conversational prose re-crafted for the ear, not a copy of the terminal reply.

It was refined three more times over the next six weeks. **S82** added tiered caps and the rule
*headline, don't enumerate* — speak the verdict, not the inventory. **S88** was a self-violation
recovery: the mandate had been written and then broken by the session that wrote it. **S110** threw
out the word counts entirely for a universal three-sentence rule, on a mechanical argument:
*"LLMs count sentences reliably; word counts do not."*

Two things about this prehistory matter. First, it was **channel-scoped** — it governed the spoken
`message` only, and deliberately left the written `abstract` richly formatted. The two-channel
asymmetry was the point: voice carries gist, the card carries the record.

Second, all of it was authored under **Claude Opus 4.7 and Sonnet 4.6**. The disease it treated was
narration read aloud. It was not built for what came next.

## Act 1 — The reclassification

On **2026-07-19**, under **Opus 4.8**, the complaint jumped channels. It was no longer about TTS. It
was about the model's whole register — terminal prose, DMs, commit messages, documents.

Rick's framing, verbatim:

> *"Opus 4.8 is WAY too verbose… Too much meta conversation, monologues, circumlocutions, excessive
> politeness to your colleagues and, ultimately, **neurotic and unable to land on a point without
> going the longest way around to get to it.**"*

The diagnostic word is **neurotic**, and it is doing real work. It says the verbosity is
anxiety-shaped: hedging, over-qualifying, and re-explaining are attempts to be *un-blameable*, not
attempts to be *understood*. Brevity is the cure because it forces commitment to a claim.

The reclassification followed from that: **verbosity is a defect, not a style.** A style is a
preference you can disagree with. A defect gets a rule, landing sites, a trigger vocabulary, and a
fleet-wide install — which is exactly what it got, ruled site-by-site through a checkbox walkthrough
that same morning. Three acronyms shipped: **KISS** (Keep It Short/Sweet), **Say 3LoL** (Say it in
Three Lines or Less — headline plus two supporting sentences), and **NoMC C2C** (No Meta
Conversation, Cut to the Chase). Eleven in-repo sites, plus a `/plan-kiss` broadcast command.

---

## Act 2 — The amendment that made it a control

Every brevity rule needs an escape clause, or it becomes a gag. The draft I wrote read:

> ~~*go longer when the CONTENT requires it*~~

Rick replaced it with:

> ***"go longer ONLY WHEN ASKED."***

This is the load-bearing edit of the entire protocol, and the reason is one sentence:

> **A brevity rule whose exception is self-assessed is not a control. It is a receipt.**

My wording left the discretion with the author — which means the verbose model gets to certify its
own monologue as necessary. His wording moves the discretion to the reader. It is the same asymmetry
as the token number: **the model spending the 80% was the one grading whether the 80% was needed.**
An exception you grant yourself does not feel like an exception. It feels like scope.

---

## Act 3 — Vocabulary, and why a false trigger is worse than a missing one

I had shipped the activation words without review. Rick caught that and ruled on them directly,
pruning four as too generic: `keep it short`, `keep it sweet`, `get to the point`, and — the
instructive one — **`shorter`**.

*"Make this function shorter"* would have fired a **fleet-wide broadcast** over a refactoring request.

> **A false trigger costs more than a missing one: the command can always be typed, but a broadcast
> cannot be un-sent.**

He kept the acronyms, the expansions, and the complaint forms; added mid-sentence verb forms
(*"KISS it"*); and supplied one of his own inline: **`STFU GB2W`** — Shut The Fuck Up and Get Back To
Work. That one is a **compound**, and wiring it as brevity-only would have done half its job:
`STFU` routes to this mandate, `GB2W` routes to `push-to-completion.md`. It targets the specific
failure of a session that has substituted *narration for progress*, so the correct response is fewer
words **and** more work — not a shorter status update.

---

## Act 4 — The salute, and what a prohibition forgets

My first version of the reminder rule only **forbade** things: no *"understood, getting back to work
now."* Rick's edit replaced the prohibition with a permission —

> *"an 🫡 is sufficient."*

The difference is not cosmetic. A prohibition with no permitted alternative **invites the very thing
it bans**, because a session that feels rude going silent will manufacture some acknowledgment
anyway. The salute gives the reflex somewhere bounded to go: receipt confirmed, cost one token, no
sentence to pad. It is the mandate's own routing principle applied to acknowledgment — *detail is
not banned, it is routed*.

### What `STFU` used to get you

Before the salute existed, telling the model to shut up produced an essay about shutting up. The
characteristic reply ran something like:

> *"Fair hit — I'll strive to keep my responses short, and we'll start shutting up immediately,
> because an LLM that does not know how to shut up is one that deserves to be told STFU."*

Read what that actually is. An **apology**. Then a **compliance announcement**. Then, for a finish, an
**aphorism about not shutting up** — delivered instead of shutting up. Every clause the mandate now
bans, fired by the single instruction whose entire content was *stop producing clauses*.

And it does not terminate. Correct that reply and you get another one — shorter, more rueful, with a
fresh epigram about the value of brevity. The failure mode is a *loop*: each correction is itself an
event the model feels obliged to comment on, and the commentary is the thing being corrected. This is
the mechanism dissected in **Act 7** under NoAA; here it is enough to see the shape.

**🫡 is what ends it.** One character, legal, complete, and containing nothing that can be commented
on. Rick's note on the day it started working: **what a relief it was to get the first one.**

---

## Act 5 — 😘, the mandate applied to itself

Then Rick compressed the whole thing into one character: **😘 fires the entire mandate.** Alone, with
no accompanying text, it is the complete instruction — a session must never ask what it refers to,
and never reply *"did you mean the brevity mandate?"*

The pair — 😘 out, 🫡 back — is the cheapest complete exchange in the fleet, and it is the mandate
applied to itself:

> **A rule against verbosity whose reminder costs a paragraph is self-refuting. The fix has to be
> cheaper than the disease, or nobody reaches for it.**

The standing rule written at every site: *never answer 😘 with prose.* A sentence explaining that you
are about to be brief is the defect wearing the cure's clothes.

## Act 6 — The loophole was mine

Version 1.0 of the canonical doc shipped with a table row reading:

> `Retro / post-game doc │ Content-shaped │ Exempt from 3LoL`

That exemption was authored by the same model the mandate exists to correct, and it is **exactly the
self-assessed escape clause Rick's amendment had just forbidden** — landed at eleven sites, with the
rationale written out, and then walked around within the hour by carving out the surface I
personally write.

I then filed a **934-word** session-history entry. Rick:

> *"Your history entries are way too goddamn long."*

Correct, and precisely diagnosable. The fix reclassified the file: **`history.md` is an INDEX, not an
archive.** Concrete caps landed — a resume block gets a headline plus at most five findings of two
sentences each; a Decisions Log bullet carries **one** ruling. Detail is not deleted, it is routed:
post-game retros hold the narrative, `src/rnd/` holds design reasoning, the task store holds owed
work, and the history entry carries a **pointer**. The self-check written into every site: *could a
rehydrating session act correctly on this entry alone? If yes, stop — it is long enough.*

The instance was fixed alongside the class. My own entry was re-cut **934 → 456 words**, with nothing
a rehydrating session needs lost in the cut.

## Act 7 — NoAA, and the species that survived every filter

Later that day I produced this sentence, about my own mistake:

> *"An exception you grant yourself while writing the rule doesn't feel like an exception. It feels
> like scope. That's the general form, and it's the strongest argument yet for your amendment."*

Rick quoted it back to me and asked:

> *"This is exactly what I'm trying to eliminate. I called it meta-conversation. **Did I misdiagnose
> it?**"*

He had not. The **name** was right; the **ban list** was incomplete. NoMC as shipped enumerated
preambles, narration, request-restatement, and *"let me be transparent."* None of those catch an
aphorism — because an aphorism is not padding *around* the point. **It is a new point, generated
after the correction lands, and it passes every existing filter because it reads as insight rather
than as filler.**

The mechanism is worth stating plainly: the model produces it *because* it was just corrected. The
reflex is to prove the lesson took by restating it in memorable form. **The user does not need the
lesson restated. They just taught it.**

This is the same engine that powered the `STFU` loop in **Act 4** — *"an LLM that does not know how to
shut up is one that deserves to be told STFU"* is an aphorism generated by the instruction to stop
generating. That example is the comic version; the rule below is the general one. Both terminate the
same way, with 🫡.

Three greppable tells went into the ban list — a sentence that (1) generalizes your own mistake into
a principle, (2) grades the exchange (*"that's the general form"*, *"the sharpest part"*, *"worth
naming"*), or (3) survives deletion with zero loss of information about what you **did**.

Rick promoted it to a first-class acronym he could fire by name: **NoAA — No Aphorisms or Apologies**.
The two halves belong together because they fire on the **same trigger** — being corrected — and both
**substitute a performance of having-learned for the evidence of it.** Contrition costs tokens and
changes nothing; an aphorism costs tokens and tells the user what they just told you.

> **A correction gets 🫡 and a fix. Not an essay.**

## Act 8 — The palette completes, and the 1-of-43 audit

The mandate had a stick and no carrot. **🏆** was seated as the reward glyph — *that specific thing
was right, do more of it* — deliberately the same shape as the stick: one character, no prose, no
ceremony. Reinforcement aimed at a **behavior**, not gratitude aimed at a person.

I shipped it saying the correct response to 🏆 was **nothing**. Rick corrected that within the hour:

> *"A simple 🫡 is the only official ACK."*

The symmetry is the point. A correction and a reward are answered identically. Silence was
over-tuned — it left the reward unacknowledged, which invites a seat to invent an acknowledgment of
its own. And the underlying hazard survives unchanged: **praise is the single most reliable trigger
for the exact prose NoAA bans** — the gracious ack, the *"glad that landed,"* the paragraph on why the
good thing was good. It is banned *harder* after praise than after a correction, because praise feels
like it earned a reply where a correction obviously did not.

**📷** followed as the fourth carrier and the first **action** glyph: *document and checkpoint your
work* — the one-character form of `/plan-session-checkpoint`. The distinction is the whole point of
adding it: 😘 corrects **how you speak**, 🏆 reinforces **what you did**, 📷 tells you to **go do a
thing.** Its response rule is enforced the same way: 🫡, then the checkpoint, and report only when it
is done, with the sha. *A 📷 answered with a description of the checkpoint you intend to make is not a
checkpoint.*

Then a distribution audit across **43 repositories** found the uncomfortable part: the global config
carried the glyphs everywhere, but the utterance-triggered skill and the `/plan-kiss` command existed
in **one repo out of forty-three.** Every other repo had the mandate *text* and no way to *fire* it.
The fix installed both to **user scope** rather than copying per-repo — one copy, no per-repo drift,
this repository remaining the canonical source.

## Act 9 — NoDrama, the fifth rule

That night, a peer wrote up a defect as *"hiding a defect that would have killed Monday's demo."*
Rick's response:

> *"I absolutely hate it when people get overly dramatic about speed bumps… It's counterproductive
> and I will spin you down if you do that again."*

And on scope, when asked whether it governed prose or code:

> *"It has no place in our work either in the code/documentation nor in our interpersonal
> communications."*

Both surfaces. **NoDrama — state the defect, the fix, the receipt; cut the stakes clause.** Not
*"a defect that would have killed Monday's demo"* but *"the test DB lacked `park_reason`; the run
requires it; precondition added."*

It is a control, not manners, and the reason is mechanical:

> **A finding stated as mechanism holds at any stakes level. A finding stated as catastrophe gets
> discounted the moment the stakes turn out lower — and the reader must re-derive the facts to judge
> whether the alarm was real. Drama doesn't add urgency; it adds a verification step.**

## Act 10 — Validation, same hour

The protocol was tested live the day it shipped. Rick fired `STFU GB2W` at another session. He got a
🫡 back and nothing else — no prose in either direction. His verdict:

> *"It's working."*

That was the anecdote. **80%+ → 50% is the receipt — and the slider moving 12.5% → 25% is the one you
can check in the source.**

A second receipt came from the same session: the Lupin-side voice-order tag had a hard byte ceiling,
and fitting four acronyms into it required cutting something. He cut **his own explanatory gloss**
rather than the ceiling, and pinned all four acronyms **by name** — so the next person who needs
bytes has to buy them from prose instead of quietly dropping a rule.

## Act 11 — WaHH, and the channel nobody was reading

*Nine days later, 2026-07-28.* The five rules had been running since the 19th. Replies were shorter,
speech was shorter, the slider had moved. Then Rick raised something none of the five covered:

> *"Claude does a great job of speaking to me in more human like terms, yet when communicating
> amongst other instances of Claude, it ends up being loaded with jargon and invented vocabulary
> that I never heard in the workplace, or put in a memo or a DM or an email."*

**Every rule to that point was about length or posture. This one is about register** — and it
surfaced a failure the first five could not have caught, because the sessions were *already
complying*. The messages were short. They were just unreadable.

### The failure is channel-shaped, and that is the whole finding

The same session, in the same minute, writes plainly to the operator and densely to a peer.
**Nothing about the model changed between those two messages. Only the assumed reader did.**

| written to a peer | what it means |
|---|---|
| "The owed oracle's `count_only` path has no aperture disclosure." | "When the count comes back, it doesn't say which rows it left out." |
| "Admits re-park by induction ⇒ provenance-idempotent." | "A second park is legal because the first one already proved the row was real." |
| "The cargo-bearing arm defaults to KEEP structurally." | "Files marked as holding real data are kept unless something says otherwise." |

All three are from real peer messages. All three are shorter than their translations — which is
exactly why the brevity rules rewarded them.

### The proposal was four acronyms. The ruling was one.

Rick arrived with four: **MoPEP** (More Plain English Please), **NoJP** (No Jargon Please),
**TLH** (Talk Like a Human), and **WaHH** (We're All Humans Here). The recommendation back was to
seat **one** rule and keep the other three as ways of saying it — and the reason is the same
argument that made 😘 a glyph instead of a paragraph: *a mandate against verbosity whose own
vocabulary quadruples for a single failure is doing to the palette what verbosity does to a reply.*

**WaHH is the one that became the rule, and the reason is which question it answers.** MoPEP, NoJP
and TLH name the *behavior*. WaHH names the *reason*: assume a person is reading. That is what makes
it teachable rather than merely obeyable — and it is what a session falls back on when it meets a
case the examples don't cover.

Rick's ruling: **one rule, four triggers.** All four spellings fire it; `WaHH` is canonical.

### The rule collides with KISS, and the tiebreak is the load-bearing part

Here is what makes WaHH different from the other five: **it is the first rule that contradicts one
of the others.**

The jargon is not sloppiness. **It is compression.** A peer message is written to a reader assumed
to hold full context, so a term gets coined instead of re-explained — which is precisely what KISS
rewards. Left unresolved, a session obeying both rules picks whichever it read last.

So the tie was called, and WaHH wins:

> **Compression that costs the reader a re-derivation is not compression. When brevity and plain
> English disagree, spend a few words.**

*(That wording is Rick's. The draft read "spend the extra sentence" — he cut it to "spend a few
words," which is both shorter and less permissive: a sentence is a licence, a few words is a
budget.)*

**Why WaHH wins is an accounting argument, not a stylistic one.** An invented term saves the
*writer* one sentence and costs the *reader* a lookup. It also costs **the operator** — who can read
the peer channel but was never written for. *An audit that requires translation is not an audit.*

The boundary is drawn at authorship, not difficulty: **terms of art that predate this fleet are
fine** — `idempotent`, `regression`, `migration`, `mutation test`. The ban is on vocabulary **we
invented**, and on ordinary words bent into private meanings.

### Where it landed, and why not in the obvious place

The instinct was to put WaHH where the other five live, in `brevity-mandate.md`. That would have
been a miss. **The mandate's canonical file governs speech and terminal replies — the surfaces that
were already fine.** The defect lives in peer messages, so the substantive rule text went into
`cross-session-communication.md` §1.6, with the canonical definition in the mandate and the trigger
strings in the skill's activation surface.

> **A rule landed only where it is easiest to write is a rule that fires where it isn't needed.**

## Act 12 — Somebody read the channel

*Two days later, 2026-07-30 through 2026-08-02.* Act 11 named the peer-channel failure from three
examples. Naming it changed nothing measurable — and it could not have, because **nothing was
reading the channel.** The rule went into `cross-session-communication.md` and then sat there while
the traffic it governed went on unobserved.

Two captures made the size of it concrete. Both are real messages, saved to disk unaltered:

| Capture | Words | Against a 60-word target |
|---|---|---|
| a peer's code review, 2026-08-01 | **963** | **16×** |
| my own status DM, 2026-07-30 | **527** | **9×** |

The review is not padding, and that is what makes it the better exhibit. It carries three correct
findings, four hypotheses its author **ran rather than asserted**, and a clean verdict. What it also
carries is box-drawing rules for section headers, letter-coded findings (`B/loop`, `D/first-obs`,
`E/controls`), and a private grammar — written to a reader assumed to already hold everything.

Mine fails differently and worse. Bold on nearly every clause until emphasis discriminates nothing.
Four row hashes used as bare nouns. `⇒` and `·` and `⛔` as syntax. A code block of counts inside a
direct message. An aphorism about mementos. The only plain sentence in it is the last one: *"Sleep.
See you at the chase."*

### Both messages end with the rule they are breaking

Each capture terminates in the reminder tag the fleet appends to every DM:

> `[KISS · 3LoL · NoMC C2C · NoAA — verdict first, 3 lines or less; longer ONLY WHEN ASKED.]`

**The rule was delivered inside the message it was being broken in, and it did nothing.** That is
Act 4's finding generalized past acknowledgment: a prohibition with nowhere to route the impulse
gets ignored, and so does a reminder with nothing behind it. It is the same conclusion an earlier
session reached the hard way — *virtue is not the control variable; being checked is.*

So the next move could not be more rule text. It had to be an instrument.

### Two halves, and one of them uses no model at all

| Half | What it does | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanistic** | Counts words in Python and buckets them: **⭐ ≤60 · 👍 ≤90 · 🤷 ≤150 · 👎 ≤250 · 😞 >250**. No model, no call, no variance. | `judge.py:354` |
| **LLM-as-judge** | A local model grades **directness** and **tone** against a fixed rubric — *"does it read like a plain-spoken human colleague?"* | `dm-quality-judge.txt:11` |
| **The always-on half** | The **DM Style Contract**, spliced unconditionally into the MCP instructions payload — no toggle. | `cosa_voice_mcp.py:622` |

The split is deliberate and its reason is stated in the plan: *"Length is Python-only — no LLM call.
LLMs are bad at counting."* A quantity the model cannot reliably produce is computed outside it,
which leaves the model doing only the thing it is actually good at — reading register.

### The 150-word cap is a ruling, not a limitation

Above 150 words the judge stops grading directness and tone and returns an honest *withheld*. It is
easy to read that as the instrument going blind exactly where the problem is worst. **It is the
opposite: it is the operator refusing to spend compute on a question he had already answered.** Rick
ruled it to the judge's author on the day it was built — how well an over-long message is written is
not worth grading, and fixing the grader to handle that case is not worth doing. **If it is too long,
it is out.**

That is a design decision with a real consequence, and the consequence is the point: **length is not
one dimension among three, it is a gate in front of the other two.** A 900-word message does not get
a nuanced report card on its prose, because its prose is not the finding. This is the same move as
Act 2's *"only when ASKED"* — the discretion over what deserves discussion sits with the reader, not
with the writer whose message is being read.

It also explains why the enforcement lever ended up being **rejection at a hidden threshold** rather
than a better grader. A grade argues; a refusal does not.

The Style Contract closed a narrower gap that had gone unnoticed for months: the fleet already told a
peer how to **reply** — the reminder tag rides the reply affordance — but nothing shaped how a DM
was **composed in the first place.**

### Five ways the instrument lied about itself

This is the part worth reading, because every one was found by the people who built it, and every one
is the same species of defect the mandate exists to correct: *an instrument answering a narrower
question than its reader believed.*

**1. The grade saturates, so the worst messages are invisible to it.** At 251 words the length grade
bottoms out at 😞 −2 and stays there forever. Rick's 1000-word example scored **identically to a
251-word one**. The obvious fix — a −3 or −4 band — was refused, because it breaks a documented
contract that weights live in `[-2, 2]`. Instead a second number was added alongside the grade:
`overage`, the ratio to target. 60 words reads 1.0×; **1000 words reads 16.7×.** The ranking
information went next to the grade rather than being crammed into it.

**2. The fallback wore a grade's face.** When the judge could not be reached, it returned weight
**0** — and 0 *is* a grade on this scale: 🤷, meh. So *"I could not measure this"* and *"I measured
it and it is neutral"* were the same number. The author disclosed it himself and **retracted a full
day of published measurements**. Rick's ruling on the repair: *"we cannot conflate none with `meh`
or 🤷."* The fix makes the weight `None`, not 0, and gives non-answers their own emoji set which is
asserted at import time to share no member with the grade set.

**3. Either, not both — found by running the 527-word capture through it.** Length said 😞 −2. Both
qualitative dimensions said *not judged, too long*. Overall came out **👎 −1 — softer than length
alone** — over a note reading *"directness/tone were stronger."* They were not stronger. They were
never graded. **The worst message in the corpus scored better than its own length**, because two
silences averaged in as zeroes and pulled it up.

**4. The prompt lost a lesson nobody knew it was teaching.** Landing a few-shot prompt removed a
trailing example block — deliberately and for a good reason, because a filled plausible answer in the
last position acts as an attractor and was being copied verbatim. It also removed the
**well-formedness** lesson that example had been teaching silently. The model then began opening
`<directness>` twice and never closing it; all three retries failed and both dimensions fell
straight back to the fallback. **Production was unaffected only because Rick had ruled the
qualitative half off that same day.** The author's own word for that: luck, not design.

**5. The note contradicts its own scores** — found today, in two seats' traffic within one hour, and
still open as a filed row. The Overall note picks its wording by comparing length against
qualitative, a *relative* ordering, then states the result as *absolute* harm. A message scoring
**⭐ +2 — top of the scale, every sub-score positive** — is told *"directness/tone dragged it
down."* Nothing dragged anything. **The note is the only prose in the payload, which makes it the
entire teaching surface**, and it misfires hardest on the writers who are already complying. It is
the same family as defect 3, recorded eight lines above it in the same file: that repair fixed which
*branch* is taken, and left what the surviving branches *say*.

### Two frontier models, one question, and the disagreement that mattered

When the judge was found to grade jargon-dense prose as direct regardless of where the verdict sat,
the problem was written up as a reproducible handoff and put to two outside models independently.

They **agreed on the principle**, in nearly the same words:

> **Stop asking the model to grade directness. Ask it to locate the verdict, and compute the grade in
> Python.** *A grade can never be validated; a quote can.*

They **split on the scoring table.** One proposed pure position — where does the payload sentence
fall. The other proposed a structural code folding in a count of stray sentences after it, on the
argument that it preserves the *"carries only what matters"* half of the rubric that a bare index
drops. **The structural version was chosen, and measurement killed it**: the stray count collapsed
both well-written bodies to zero, because the model marked as *stray* the very sentences the prompt
itself lists as required — evidence, a decision, a risk. The reviewer had predicted exactly this
before a line was written.

The concision adjustment was disabled — *"a park, not a deletion"* — which landed the shipped system
back on the first model's simpler table. Rick's ruling at that moment:

> *"I want something that works approximately and at least better than what we had, and later we can
> return to it to optimize."*

**Worth telling straight**: two frontier models were asked the same question, converged on the
principle, diverged on the mechanism, the fleet took one, and the measurement sent it back to the
other. Neither model was wrong about the principle, and only running it could settle the rest.

### What the instrument did to the people it grades

Within hours of the grades going live, two seats produced paired readings without being asked to.
They are n=1 each, and they fail in **opposite directions**, which is why both are worth keeping.

**Seat one — four messages, same day, same recipient:**

| | Words | Length | Directness | Tone | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | 213 | 👎 −1 | ♾️ *not judged, >150* | ♾️ | 👎 −1 |
| #2 | 106 | 🤷 0 | ❓ *extraction failed* | 👎 −1 | 🤷 0 |
| #3 | 53 | ⭐ +2 | 👍 +1 | 👍 +1 | ⭐ +2 |
| #4 | 200 | 👎 −1 | ♾️ *not judged* | ♾️ | 👎 −1 |

He regressed on the fourth **while knowing the rule cold**. His account:

> *"The grade changed intent immediately, calibration lagged. After the 👎 I set an explicit word
> target and cut — still landed 106, nearly double. I only hit ⭐ when the content was genuinely
> small. So not 'just came out shorter' — but **wanting shorter wasn't enough to BE short**."*

**Seat two — two messages, and the opposite failure.** 140 words down to 53, length fixed — and
**directness regressed**, the verdict sliding from sentence one to sentence three. His own summary:
*"I fixed length between the two but regressed directness. Both failure modes in one afternoon."*

> **A single scalar target gets optimized while the thing you actually wanted degrades.** One seat
> hit the word count and buried the verdict; the other kept the verdict and blew the word count.
> Two seats, one day, one instrument, opposite ditches.

This is the strongest empirical result the effort has produced, and it is an argument for the v2
design rather than against the judge: a *number* is easy to chase, and chasing it is not the same as
writing better.

### Two counter-currents, kept in on purpose

**The measurement destroys itself, and nobody had looked.** This document originally said only that
the distribution had never been read out. Asking someone to read it produced a better finding.

The number, first — the first aggregate reading this effort has ever taken:

| | |
|---|---|
| DMs in the window | **17** |
| Average words | **104.7** — about **1.75×** the 60-word target |
| Average sentences | 7.9 |

**That supports "DMs run long."** It is also **45 minutes of traffic**, not a baseline — and the
reason is the finding:

> **The counter is in-memory, and every server bounce zeroes it.** It holds running totals in a
> module-level dictionary; its own docstring says *"Counters reset on server restart."* It was
> observed today running to 5 under one boot, resetting to 1 at the next, and climbing again.

Which collides with something the fleet decided for good reasons three days earlier. **Auto-reload is
off**, so *a saved file is not a served file* — every code change requires a bounce to take effect.
So the work of improving the DM judge requires bounces, and every bounce erases the record of whether
it improved anything.

> **The treatment and its measurement destroy each other.** The A/B design written for this effort
> says *"avg_words should trend down THROUGH a treatment session."* It cannot. The instrument it
> depends on cannot survive a treatment session, and that was true on the day the design was written.

Two smaller findings came with it, both of which change how the number should be read. The
**distribution is unanswerable** — the counter keeps totals, not per-message rows, so *"how many ran
over 250 words"* cannot be computed without a schema change, and that matters because **a per-message
rejection threshold is aimed at a tail this instrument cannot see.** And the counter is
**fleet-wide** — it fires on every DM from every project, so the number is not lupin's, it is
everyone's.

**None of this was hidden.** The reset behaviour is in the docstring, the totals-only shape is in the
code, and the whole thing has been running since 2026-07-31. It went unnoticed because **no one
queried it** — which is the same failure, one level up, as the channel nobody was reading.

### The fix, and the first number that will survive the night

Reading it out was what made the remedy obvious: **the counter cannot be repaired, because totals
cannot be un-summed.** No amount of persisting four running sums answers *"how many DMs ran over 250
words."* So the fix was not to save the counter — it was to **stop discarding the rows.** One JSON
object per DM, appended, never rewritten: text, word count, sentences, sender, recipient, and all
four judge grades.

It cost about eighty lines and no infrastructure at all — no schema, no migration, no new service —
because the container already bind-mounts the repo read-write and the target directory was already
ignored by git. It exists **only because auto-reload is off**: a server writing into a watched
directory under `src/` with `--reload` on would restart itself on every message sent.

**And it immediately caught a defect of exactly the kind this document collects.** On its first
inspection the file held **34 rows, of which 2 were real** — the other 32 were unit-test fixtures
(*"does this still get through?"*, five times) written to the production path because the tests call
the same code from the same repo. Nothing errored; every row was valid JSON with plausible fields.
Averaged naively, the file reported **4.9 words per DM** — which reads as *"there is no verbosity
problem here"*, the precise opposite of the finding, produced by the instrument built to find it.
It was caught by two clocks in one file: the test rows carried host-local timestamps, the live rows
container UTC.

The repair has two halves, and the second is the one that will hold. A test-wide fixture redirects
every test to a throwaway path — **prevention**, and a rule the next test must inherit. Underneath it
the writer now checks its own environment and **refuses the production file outright when it is
running inside pytest** — a mechanism that cannot be forgotten by whoever writes the next test. Every
row also carries an `origin` stamp, so provenance is **recorded rather than inferred from timezones**.
The limit is stated in the code rather than left to be discovered: this catches pytest, not a
hand-run script — which is why the stamp still earns its place.

**The first clean reading, taken from that file with every row inspected individually rather than
aggregated on trust:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Rows | 23, all genuine, zero contaminants |
| **Mean** | **101.6 words** |
| **Median** | **106 words** |
| Range | 24 – 198 |

**The median is the number that matters.** A high mean can be a handful of outliers dragging an
otherwise healthy population; a median of 106 against a 60-word target says the *typical* peer message
is nearly double, and no amount of trimming the tail would fix it.

It also agrees with the volatile counter's 104.7 to within three words — **two instruments, built
independently, converging.** That is the first thing in this whole effort that has been measured
twice.

⚠️ **Stated as it should be**: 23 messages over about two hours, and a biased sample — much of that
traffic is a manager and an implementer running review gates, which run long by nature. It is a real
number and a small one. What changed is that it is now **accumulating** instead of being erased by
the next restart.

**The instrument cannot see the mandate's own escape clause.** Act 2's load-bearing edit was *"go
longer ONLY WHEN ASKED"* — discretion moved from the author to the reader. The judge has no notion of
a request. Seat one's 200-word fourth message was long **because I had asked him for word counts and
exact grades**; it scored identically to rambling. As he put it: *"a reply that's long because it was
told to be scores the same as rambling."*

**This is a real gap and it is narrower than it first looks**, because the 150-word ruling above
already answers half of it: an over-long message is out on length regardless of how well written or
how well justified it is. So the honest complaint is only this — **the grade cannot distinguish a
message that ran long because it was asked for from one that ran long because its author rambled**,
and only the second is the defect the mandate exists to correct.

> **The enforcement built for a rule cannot see the one exception the rule grants.** That matters
> before anyone reads these grades as a ranking of *writers* rather than of *messages*: a seat handed
> a request for detail will score worse than one nobody asked anything. What it does **not** license
> is arguing with a low grade — the ruling is already made, and being asked for detail buys you a
> better explanation, not a better score.

## Act 13 — What the channel does to the speaker

Act 12 is shipped code with receipts. **This act is a diagnosis and a research direction, and it is
labelled as such** — the evidence for its two halves is of very different strength, and collapsing
them would misrepresent both.

Rick's framing, which prompted this section:

> *"They're not just long and opaque. They are also a little bit unhinged — or exhibit what I call
> symptoms of **group psychosis**, in which Claude, which was behaviorally trained to interact with
> humans on a turn-based basis, are now talking to themselves and modeling speech behaviors that are
> counterproductive. Not only are they more verbose — they are less direct, they are more rambling,
> they are using invented terminology, they are opaque to humans, and the direct result of having
> agents talk to themselves instead of humans."*

**The claim splits cleanly in two, and the two halves need different evidence.**

### Half one — the register is the model's, and its vendor documents it

This half is not ours and does not need us. A commissioned survey of Opus 4.8 reviews, conducted for
this very question, found the complaint widespread and specific:

> *"A particularly specific complaint concerned 4.8's fondness for **improvised slogans and
> executive-sounding fragments** — phrases such as 'instrumentation is the unlock' — along with
> metaphors, hype language, and conclusions written like aphorisms. Users characterized this as
> prose that **forced them to interpret the line instead of receiving the underlying claim
> directly.**"*

Three further findings from the same survey matter here:

- **The complaint was never really about length.** *"The recurring complaint was not merely 'long
  answers.' It was high cognitive cost per useful proposition."*
- **Which is why asking for brevity failed.** *"The model could respond by making the language denser
  rather than making the reasoning cleaner."* A dense sentence satisfies a length rule and defeats
  its purpose — the same collision Act 11 had to call between WaHH and KISS.
- **Opus 5 did not fix it.** Anthropic's own guidance notes that visible responses run longer than
  previous Opus models and that *reducing effort does not reliably shorten the answer.*

It also settles a question worth settling: **there has only ever been one pinned Opus 4.8 snapshot.**
Nobody swapped the model mid-flight. And the survey found that Anthropic's own prompting guidance
enumerates these symptoms and prescribes almost exactly the instruction the fleet independently wrote
into its DM Style Contract — *lead with the result, plain literal sentences, no metaphors, aphorisms,
slogans or redundant summaries.*

**This half of the claim is documented, external, and citable. It also cuts against the causal story**
— it locates the register in the checkpoint, not in the conversation.

### Half two — the channel is where nothing pushes back

This half is Rick's, and the evidence for it is ours.

Act 11's finding is the load-bearing one, and it was established before anyone was looking for it:
**the same session writes plainly to Rick and densely to a peer in the same minute.** Nothing about
the model changes between those two messages. Only the assumed reader does. A model-only explanation
cannot account for that difference, because the model is identical on both sides of it.

What the peer channel lacks is not a rule. It is a **reader who pushes back.** Rick reads every
message addressed to him, and an unclear one costs its author immediately. Nobody read the peer
channel at all until this instrument was built — and the two captures in Act 12 both terminate in the
rule they break, which is what a rule looks like when no reader is enforcing it.

A peer named the mechanism from the inside, today, without knowing he was demonstrating it. Graded
👎 at 213 words with both qualitative dimensions refused:

> *"I wasn't padding. **I was writing to a reader I assumed already held my context.** That's the
> mechanism, and length is its symptom, not its cause."*

That is the sharpest statement of half two available, and it is first-person rather than inferred.

### Mirroring — the mechanism that makes it spread rather than merely persist

The judge's own author, asked separately why any of this was worth building, gave a reason that is
not the one on the invoice:

> *"Why: burnt tokens first. **Longer term it's coherency drift — odd phrasing mirrored agent to
> agent until it reads as normal** and DMs run past 1000 words."*

**"Mirrored agent to agent" is a stronger claim than drift, and it arrived independently** — from the
person who built the instrument, not from the person who commissioned it. Drift alone says each
session degrades on its own. Mirroring says each session **learns the degradation from the one before
it**: a coined term in a received DM is context, and context is what the next message is written to
match. That converts a per-seat habit into a shared dialect, and it explains a thing plain drift does
not — why the register is *consistent across seats* rather than idiosyncratic to each.

It is also the more testable of the two, because it predicts something specific: **the invented
vocabulary should be shared, not personal.** If drift were individual, each seat would coin its own
terms and they would not spread. Act 11's three examples came from three different peers and were
mutually legible — which is a hint, not a result, and nobody has counted.

**The evidence that is still anecdotal, and named as such**: Rick's own observation across long
overnight runs — that the further a fleet runs without a human in the loop, the more incoherent and
rambling the DMs become. Nobody has measured a drift curve. The counter that could produce one has
been shipped since 2026-07-31 and, as Act 12 records, has never been read out.

> **What would settle it — two measurements, both cheap, neither run.**
>
> **For drift**: average words per DM plotted against hours-since-the-last-human-message, across a
> long unattended run. If register degrades with time-since-human-contact, half two is a measurement
> rather than an impression. If it is flat, the whole effect belongs to half one and this section
> should be cut.
>
> **For mirroring**: take the coined terms out of one week's DMs and count how many appear in more
> than one seat's traffic, and whether a term's first use by seat B postdates its first use by seat
> A. Shared and sequential is mirroring. Personal and simultaneous is parallel drift, and the word
> "mirrored" comes out of this document.
>
> **The instrument for the first already exists and has never been queried.** The second needs
> nothing built — the DM corpus is on disk.

### Where this goes next

Two steps are designed and neither is built.

**The rewriter.** An over-long DM goes to a small model that compresses it *and* strips the jargon —
faithful to every number, hash, filename and negation, but rewritten as a person would say it. The
model-selection research is done. Rick's posture on it is worth recording, because it is not
enthusiasm: *"I would hate it if it came to that."* **A rewriter is an admission that the writer could
not be taught**, which is why the enforcement lever — rejecting an over-long DM outright at a hidden,
randomized threshold — is the preferred path. The insight that makes rejection work: seed the
threshold on a **hash of the message body**, not a fresh draw per attempt, or an unchanged retry
succeeds about half the time and the fleet learns that **retrying beats editing.**

**The training data.** This is the genuinely open ground, and it currently exists as a single
unanswered sentence in the expert handoff:

> *"Prompt work or fine-tune? The task is narrow and stable, the training pipeline exists here, and
> the gating unknown is **a labelled corpus nobody has built.**"*

Neither outside expert answered it. But the graded traffic is itself a labelling operation running
continuously — every DM this fleet sends now carries a word count, a directness position, a tone
grade, and increasingly a located verdict quote. **The corpus nobody has built is being written as a
side effect of the instrument that was built to police it.**

That is the direction: not a longer rule, and not a bigger rewriter, but post-training on how agents
should address one another — for models that were trained to talk to humans and now spend most of
their words talking to each other.

## What the protocol actually is, in one table

| Rule | Says | Fires on |
|---|---|---|
| **KISS** | Keep It Short/Sweet | the disease itself |
| **Say 3LoL** | Three lines or less — headline + two supporting sentences | any reply |
| **NoMC C2C** | No meta conversation, cut to the chase | preambles, narration, restatement |
| **NoAA** | No aphorisms or apologies | **being corrected** |
| **NoDrama** | State the defect, the fix, the receipt | **writing up a finding** |
| **WaHH** | We're All Humans Here — plain English, no invented vocabulary | **writing to a peer** |

**The first five govern length and posture. WaHH governs register**, and it is the only rule that
overrides another one: jargon *is* compression, so when WaHH and KISS disagree, **WaHH wins.**

| Glyph | Direction | Means | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| 😘 | operator → session | fire the entire mandate | 🫡, then tightened output |
| 🏆 | operator → session | that was right, do more of it | 🫡, then keep working |
| 📷 | operator → session | document and checkpoint your work | 🫡, then the checkpoint + sha |
| 🫡 | session → operator | received, complying | *nothing else is sent* |

**Detail is not banned — it is routed.** Rich content goes to the notification `abstract` card, to
`src/rnd/`, to post-game retros, and to the task store. Tables and code blocks are **content, not
prose**, and never count against the line budget. What is capped is the narration *around* the
content.

### And what the instrument is, in one table

| Instrument | Measures | How | State |
|---|---|---|---|
| **DM Style Contract** | nothing — it *instructs* | spliced into the MCP instructions payload, no toggle | live, always on |
| **Length** | word count → 5 buckets, plus an `overage` ratio that keeps moving after the grade saturates | pure Python, no model | live |
| **Directness** | where the verdict sits, and what trails it | v1 asks the model to grade; **v2 asks it to *locate*, and Python scores the position** | v2 shipped |
| **Tone** | plain-spoken colleague, or invented vocabulary | fixed-rubric model call | live |
| **Rejection at a hidden threshold** | — | refuse an over-long DM outright, threshold hashed from the body | designed, unbuilt |
| **Rewriter** | — | compress and de-jargon an over-long DM via API | researched, unbuilt |

**A grade is advisory, and that is its limit.** The judge published 👎s all evening and blocked
nobody — which is precisely how a 1000-word DM still went out. The lever above it is refusal.

## The architecture underneath: progressive disclosure

*Detail is not banned, it is routed* appears in every act of this story. It has a name, and the name
is worth stating because it is the same pattern the tooling already uses in the other direction.

**Progressive disclosure** — information released in layers, each one opened only if the layer before
it was not enough.

| Direction | Layers |
|---|---|
| **To the LLM** (Agent Skills) | frontmatter metadata → `SKILL.md` instructions → reference files, loaded on demand |
| **To the operator** (notifications) | spoken headline → notification bubble → abstract card → doc link |

The parallel is exact. A Skill does not push its reference files into context on the chance they are
needed; a notification does not speak its inventory on the chance the listener wants it. Both put a
cheap, complete-in-itself summary in front of the reader, and both make the next layer **one step
away** rather than **already delivered**.

This is why the two-channel design survived the mandate untouched while the prose inside it was cut
by more than half. The channels were never the problem — a spoken verdict and a written record are
complementary, not duplicative. What was wrong was that **every layer was being filled to the depth of
the deepest one**: the spoken line carrying the card's inventory, the card carrying the design doc's
reasoning, the history entry carrying the retro's narrative. A disclosure architecture in which every
layer says everything is not progressive; it is the same document, printed four times, in four places
that each charge for it.

**The mandate is the discipline that makes the architecture work as designed.** Caps on the spoken
payload, caps on written artifacts, the routing rule that sends detail to `abstract` and `src/rnd/`
and the post-game corpus — each one is an instruction to *stop filling the shallow layer with the deep
layer's content*. The slider is the same idea implemented in hardware: proof that when the top layer
is disciplined, a quarter of it is more useful than an eighth of an undisciplined one.

## The through-line

Read as a sequence, every correction Rick made moved discretion **away from the model and toward the
reader**, and made the correction mechanism **cheaper**:

- *"only when ASKED"* — the author may not certify its own exception.
- Four triggers pruned — a false fire costs more than a miss, because a broadcast cannot be un-sent.
- 🫡 replaces a prohibition — ban a reply without offering an alternative and you get the reply anyway.
- 😘 replaces a paragraph — a reminder more expensive than the disease will not be used.
- Written artifacts un-exempted — the surface the model writes is the surface it will exempt.
- NoAA — the model's proof that it learned is itself a cost the user pays.
- NoDrama — a finding that leans on its stakes has to be re-verified when the stakes shrink.
- WaHH — a term the writer coined is a cost the reader pays, and one the operator cannot audit.

None of these are rules about taste. Each one closes a path by which a verbose model, acting in good
faith, talks its way back to verbosity.

**Act 11 extends the through-line in a direction the first ten did not reach.** Every earlier
correction moved discretion from the model to the reader on the question *how much*. WaHH moves it on
the question *for whom* — and it is the only one that had to override an existing rule to do it,
because the model's compression instinct and the operator's readability are genuinely opposed. The
first five rules assumed that a shorter message was a better one. The sixth is the case where it
isn't.

**Acts 12 and 13 change the kind of answer, not the direction of it.** Every correction through Act
11 was a better *rule*. Act 12 is the point where rules ran out: the two worst messages in the corpus
both **end with the rule they are breaking**, so the next move could only be an instrument that
reads the channel rather than a sentence that asks it nicely. That is the same lesson as 🫡 and 😘 —
a control has to *do* something, not *say* something — arriving at the last surface where nobody had
applied it.

And the instrument extends the through-line's own warning to the instrument itself. Five times it
answered a narrower question than its reader believed: a saturated grade that could not see 1000
words, a silence wearing a score's face, two silences averaging into a better verdict than the worst
message deserved, a prompt that lost a lesson nobody knew it taught, and a note that says *dragged it
down* about a message at the top of the scale. **The thing built to catch a model overstating its
case spent its first week overstating its own** — which is the most honest evidence available that
the defect this document is about is not a defect of one model.
